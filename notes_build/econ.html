1 · Labour Market Equilibrium & Wage Determination
P25 Q1a(i,viii,x,xvii),b(i),c(ii,iv),e(i),Q2a,Q3b,Q4b,Q5a,Q6c · P24 Q1a(i,viii,ix,xvii),c(ii),Q2a,Q4b,Q5a,c,Q6c

Labour and its types

  • Labour = effort of any kind (manual or mental, skilled or unskilled) put in by human beings in the production of goods and services to earn income P25 Q1b(i): labour. The service is labour; the person is the labourer (a doctor is a labourer; treating a patient is labour).
  • Wage = payment by an employer to a worker for the services in production; an expense for the employer and an income for the worker; in cash or kind.

Professionaladvanced study and special trainingdoctor, teacher, architect, pilot, lawyer, accountant, software developer P25 Q1c(ii): a P24 Q2a
  • Dignity of labour: all jobs, manual or intellectual, are respectable and equally important; none is superior or inferior. Treating manual workers as inferior violates it P25 Q1a(xvii): A.

Demand for labour

Demand for labour = number of workers an employer is willing and able to hire at a given wage rate in a given period.

  • Wage rate: inverse relation — wage falls, demand rises; wage rises, demand falls (downward-sloping curve). Table: Nu 600 → 1 worker, 500 → 2, 400 → 3, 300 → 4, 200 → 5.
  • Demand for goods and services: labour is wanted for the goods it makes, so it is a derived demand (demand that arises from the demand for other products) P24 Q1c(ii): e. A boom (more demand for goods) → firms expand → hire more labour P24 Q1a(viii) P24 Q5a.
  • Productivity of labour: more productive (trained, skilled) workers contribute more, so demand rises; training, seminars and workshops raise productivity P25 Q5a.
  • MRP: Marginal Product (MP) = extra output from one more worker = change in total production. MRP = MP × MR (price of one unit). A profit-maximising firm hires up to the point where MRP = wage (hire while MRP ≥ wage) P24 Q1a(xvii): D.

Supply of labour

Supply of labour = number of people willing and able to work at the prevailing wage in a given period.

  • Wage rate: positive relation — higher wage, more workers supplied (Nu 500 → 1, 600 → 2, 700 → 3, 800 → 4, 900 → 5).
  • Facilities and working environment: good conditions, friendly colleagues, good management attract workers (supply rises); poor conditions make workers leave P24 Q1a(i): C P25 Q1a(viii): C.
  • Other (for answers): qualifications and training needed, working hours, job security, benefits, population size, migration.

Equilibrium wage rate

  • Equilibrium is where the D and S curves meet: the equilibrium wage rate (OW) and equilibrium quantity (ON); quantity demanded = quantity supplied P25 Q1a(i): A.
  • Above E (wage W1): excess supply of labour (unemployment) → wage falls. Below E (wage W2): excess demand (shortage) → wage rises.

Households provide the factors; firms pay these incomes. All factor incomes together = national income P24 Q1b(v).

Rent

  • Rent: payment for hiring durable goods (house, land, car, furniture). In economics it extends to every factor with an inelastic (nearly fixed) supply — supply that stays the same despite a change in price P24 Q1c(iv): b.
  • Factors affecting rent: (a) demand and supply of the property (more demand than supply → rent rises); (b) location (near school, hospital, town, road, market); (c) facilities (furnished, modern amenities, safety, age and type of building, cost of construction).

Investment and saving

  • Investment = creation of new capital goods (machinery, buildings, factories); also purchase of shares and bonds; spending on education and health is investment in human capital.
  • Saving = income not spent on current consumption = disposable income − consumption expenditure. Higher income and higher interest rate → more saving.
  • Why save: financial security (old age, unemployment, disability); contingencies (sickness, death, loss of property); future investment (business, education, house); interest (reward; propensity to save rises with the rate).
  • Hoarding = unspent income that earns no interest (cash in a locker or wallet); saving = unspent income that earns interest (bank deposit).
  • Too much saving reduces demand → lower profit → less production, closures and unemployment (“Know more” in the textbook).

Schemes in Bhutan: Piggy Bank (BNB) and Child Money Back Policy (RICBL) encourage saving habits P25 Q1b(v): saving. Minor’s savings account for under 18.

Interest

  • Gross interest = whole payment to the lender. Net (pure) interest = gross interest − (expenses on risk + expenses on management).
  • Why borrowers pay interest: (i) lender gives up (abstains from) current consumption; (ii) risk that the borrower may not repay; (iii) money loses value over time (prices rise).
  • Simple interest SI = P × R × T100 (on the principal only). Compound interest: A = P(1 + R100)n, CI = A − P (“interest on interest”). Nu 1000 at 10%: SI = 100, 200, 300; CI = 100, 210, 331 (years 1, 2, 3).
  • Determination of interest: demand for capital (investment, inverse to the rate) meets supply of capital (saving, direct to the rate). Rate rises above equilibrium → excess supply → rate falls; rate falls below → excess demand → rate rises.
  • Lower interest on borrowing → more investment; higher interest on deposits → more saving P25 Q1c(i): d.

Profit

  • Profit = Total revenue − Total cost (revenue = quantity × price). It is the main motive of business and moves resources to goods in higher demand (e.g. more tourists → more tourism businesses).
  • But profit should not be the only motive: business ethics (honesty, trust, responsibility, reliability, paying tax, not selling expired goods, giving back change) and social responsibility P24 Q1a(xviii): C.

Solved questions — short answers and Section B

P25 Q1e(ii) [2] Can a ceiling on rent help housing problems? Yes, to some extent. (1) It keeps houses affordable for low- and middle-income families and stops landlords charging unfairly high rent in towns like Thimphu. (2) It protects tenants from sudden rent rises, giving security. (But a ceiling that is too low discourages owners from building or maintaining houses, so supply must also grow.)
P24 Q1e(ii) [2] Is buying a house wise for a retired civil servant who rents in Thimphu? Yes, if he can afford it. (1) Rent keeps rising and buys no asset; a house is an asset that keeps its value and gives security and stops future rent payments. (2) Rent is a recurring cost on a fixed pension, so owning reduces monthly expenses. (Caution: it needs a large sum or loan, interest and upkeep, so he should not use up all his savings.)

3 · The Economy of Bhutan
P25 Q1a(iii,v,ix,xiii),d(i),e(iii) · P24 Q1a(iv,xi,xiii,xix),Q1e(iii),Q2b,c,Q3c,d,Q7b

Features of the Bhutanese economy

  • Mixed economy: public and private sectors exist together. The private sector cannot work alone: the government frames policies and provides public goods.
  • Per capita income (GDP per capita) has been rising (about US$ 2,585 in 2013 to 3,173 in 2018 in the textbook) — a sign of growth.
  • Capital formation (creation of capital goods) has increased over the years, mainly through construction, machinery and equipment.
  • Sectors: primary (agriculture, forestry), secondary (industry, hydropower, construction), tertiary (services, tourism). The tertiary sector is the largest contributor to GDP; the primary sector contributes the least (about 15%).
  • Population: small, 727,145 (PHCB 2017); growth about 1.3% a year; the 65-and-above group is small (43,064). Challenges: growing youth unemployment, dependence on aid and imports, dependence on hydropower and tourism.
  • The shift from the primary to manufacturing and service sectors shows a transition from a traditional to a modern economy.

Economic diversification

Shifting from a single source of income to several sources in more sectors and markets. It reduces dependence and risk, raises productivity and cuts unemployment. Bhutan relies on a few sectors (hydropower, tourism), so Cottage and Small Industries (CSI) are a key area. Supporting steps: tax incentives for start-ups, easy credit, training P24 Q1a(xiii): B. Benefit: economic stability and better job opportunities in the long run (not an immediate effect) P25 Q1a(ix): D.

Consumption, saving and investment

  • Income = Consumption + Saving. More consumption → less saving; less consumption → more saving; if all income is consumed, saving is zero. Without saving there is no investment; investment is higher when savings are enough P24 Q1a(xi): A (check).
  • A balance between consumption and saving is needed: too much saving (with investment constant) lowers demand and economic activity P25 Q1a(xiii): B.
  • Ways to raise saving and investment: (a) set saving goals; (b) advocacy and awareness programmes; (c) interest rate — high on savings, low on borrowing P24 Q1a(xix): D; (d) different saving schemes for different motives.

Meaning and components

Public finance = the income (revenue) and expenditure of the government at all levels (central, district, local). Its three components are budget, public revenue and public expenditure P24 Q1d(iv): TRUE.

Circular flow of income

  • Shows the flow of payments and receipts between sectors: households, business firms, government and the rest of the world. Two-sector model: households sell factor services and receive factor income (wage, rent, interest, profit = national income); they spend it on goods and services (consumption expenditure).
  • Real flow = factor services and goods and services; money flow = payments and receipts (wages, expenditure). Households providing labour to a firm is a real flow P24 Q1a(xx): D; paying wages or buying groceries are money flows.
  • With a capital market: saving flows from households to the capital market and investment flows to firms. Equilibrium when saving = investment; if saving > investment, demand and economic activity fall.

6 · Measurement of Economic Performance & GNH
P25 Q1a(vi,xiv,xviii,xix),b(iv),Q1e(vii),Q2b,Q3d,Q6d · P24 Q1a(vi,xiv),b(iv),Q1e(i,vii),Q6a,Q6d

Economic indicators

  • An economic indicator is a statistic about the economy: unemployment, current account, income inequality, GDP per capita, education, longevity.
  • Unemployment: people in the labour force without a paid job but willing and available to work; lower unemployment = better performance.
  • Current account: exports and imports of goods and services in a year; a favourable (surplus) account = better performance.
  • Income inequality: gap in income distribution; lower inequality = better performance.
  • Human Development Index (HDI): scale 0 to 1, three dimensions: longevity (life expectancy at birth; health, spending on healthy food and exercise P25 Q1b(iv): longevity), education (adult literacy and school enrolment) and GDP per capita. Broader than GDP.

Pro-family economic approach

A policy that puts the wellbeing of families first, believing productivity rises when families are supported. Examples: paid maternity leave, higher minimum wage, paid day-care, free school supplies, equal access to education, training and health care, income enough to save.

Gross National Happiness (GNH)

  • Idea of the Fourth King (1970s); in the Constitution; a holistic approach balancing material and non-material wellbeing; “GNH is more important than GDP.” Development has been aligned with GNH since the 9th Five Year Plan. GDP measures economic prosperity; GNH measures overall wellbeing P24 Q1a(xiv): D.

7 · Balance of Payments & Exchange Rate
P25 Q1a(vii,xv),Q2d,Q4d,Q6a · P24 Q1a(xv),Q2d,Q4d,Q7a

Balance of payments (BoP)

BoP = a systematic record of all economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world in a year: receipts for exports, services and capital received; payments for imports, services and capital sent out.

  • Current account: value of goods and services, income and current transfers. Capital account: capital transfers and non-produced, non-financial assets. Financial account: direct investment, portfolio investment, reserve assets. A deficit in the current account must be met by a surplus on the capital account.
  • Surplus (favourable): receipts (credit) > payments (debit). Deficit (unfavourable): payments > receipts. In accounting terms the BoP always balances; in reality it is in surplus or deficit.
  • Consequences of a big deficit: demand for foreign exchange exceeds supply → depreciation of the domestic currency, fall in foreign exchange reserves, more borrowing and debt, higher prices of imports P25 Q1a(xv): D.

Balance of trade (BoT)

Balance of tradeBalance of payments
Coversonly exports and imports of goods (visible) and servicesall transactions: trade, services, income, transfers, capital and financial flows
Part ofa component of the current accountthe whole record (current + capital + financial)
Resultfavourable if exports > imports; unfavourable if imports > exportssurplus or deficit

Net export = exports (X) − imports (M). Negative = trade deficit.

Exchange rate

  • Exchange rate = the rate at which one currency is exchanged for another (the price of a foreign currency in our currency), set by demand for and supply of currency; it changes daily.
  • Appreciation = the value of our currency rises against another (we get more foreign currency per Nu). Depreciation = value falls.
  • Depreciation: imports become expensive (goods and services cost more at home) and exports become cheap for foreigners P25 Q1a(vii): C. Appreciation: imports cheap, exports expensive.
  • Roles: needed for international payments; a sign of economic health; affects trade; a stable rate attracts foreign capital and helps diversification.
  • Conversion: foreign amount × rate = amount in Nu.

Importance of foreign exchange

Foreign currency earned by exports is needed to pay for imports (fuel, machines, medicines), repay foreign loans, keep reserves and a stable exchange rate, finance development and study abroad.
