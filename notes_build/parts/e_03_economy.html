Features of the Bhutanese economy

  • Mixed economy: public and private sectors exist together. The private sector cannot work alone: the government frames policies and provides public goods.
  • Per capita income (GDP per capita) has been rising (about US$ 2,585 in 2013 to 3,173 in 2018 in the textbook) — a sign of growth.
  • Capital formation (creation of capital goods) has increased over the years, mainly through construction, machinery and equipment.
  • Sectors: primary (agriculture, forestry), secondary (industry, hydropower, construction), tertiary (services, tourism). The tertiary sector is the largest contributor to GDP; the primary sector contributes the least (about 15%).
  • Population: small, 727,145 (PHCB 2017); growth about 1.3% a year; the 65-and-above group is small (43,064). Challenges: growing youth unemployment, dependence on aid and imports, dependence on hydropower and tourism.
  • The shift from the primary to manufacturing and service sectors shows a transition from a traditional to a modern economy.

Economic diversification

Shifting from a single source of income to several sources in more sectors and markets. It reduces dependence and risk, raises productivity and cuts unemployment. Bhutan relies on a few sectors (hydropower, tourism), so Cottage and Small Industries (CSI) are a key area. Supporting steps: tax incentives for start-ups, easy credit, training [[P24 Q1a(xiii): B]]. Benefit: economic stability and better job opportunities in the long run (not an immediate effect) [[P25 Q1a(ix): D]].

Consumption, saving and investment

  • Income = Consumption + Saving. More consumption → less saving; less consumption → more saving; if all income is consumed, saving is zero. Without saving there is no investment; investment is higher when savings are enough [[P24 Q1a(xi): A]] (check).
  • A balance between consumption and saving is needed: too much saving (with investment constant) lowers demand and economic activity [[P25 Q1a(xiii): B]].
  • Ways to raise saving and investment: (a) set saving goals; (b) advocacy and awareness programmes; (c) interest rate — high on savings, low on borrowing [[P24 Q1a(xix): D]]; (d) different saving schemes for different motives.

Solved questions — short answers and Section B

Strategies for private sector development: (1) easy access to credit at low interest and tax incentives for new businesses; (2) simplify licensing and rules, build roads, power and internet, and train skilled workers / link producers to markets. [2 × 1] Primary sector growth is −1.15% (lowest). Measures: (1) modern farming methods: irrigation, improved seeds, machines, and training for farmers; (2) market access and value addition: farm roads, cold storage, agro-processing and fair prices; (also reduce wildlife damage). [2] Why the private sector contributes little: (1) small domestic market and a small population limit demand; (2) shortage of capital and credit, with high interest and collateral demands; (3) high cost of doing business: difficult terrain, poor transport, import dependence and a shortage of skilled labour; (also the government/public sector dominates and the private sector is risk-averse). [3 × 1] Improving the business environment: (1) simplify procedures (one-stop, online licences, fewer permits) and enforce contracts fairly, with less corruption; (2) give access to finance, infrastructure and skilled labour and offer tax incentives to start-ups and exporters. [2] Are Bhutan’s trade policies favourable for the private sector? Largely yes, with limits. (1) Duty-free trade with India and membership of SAFTA/BIMSTEC give a large market and cheap raw material; (2) incentives and an open FDI/export policy for exporters help firms; (3) But dependence on India, high transport costs (landlocked) and import-dependence make local firms struggle to compete; so policies need more support. [3 × 1] Nu 10,000 a month for every third child until age three: (1) raises the birth rate and helps the population and the future labour force grow (support for ageing and small population); (2) reduces the financial burden on families, so children get better food, health care and early education, giving a healthier workforce. [2] Social benefits of a highway linking rural areas to towns: (1) easy access to hospitals, schools and services for rural people (emergency patients reach in time); (2) farm products reach markets, raising rural income and reducing regional disparity and rural–urban migration; (3) jobs during construction and later in tourism and trade; more social and cultural contact.
