1 · Indus Valley Civilisation
P24 Q1a(xiii),b(iii),e(i),Q2b · P25 Q1e(v),Q6b

Key ideas

  • Time & place: c. 3300–1300 BCE (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) along the Indus and Ghaggar–Hakra rivers in present Pakistan and north-west India. Main sites: Harappa (found 1921), Mohenjo-daro (1922), Lothal (dockyard), Kalibangan, Dholavira. It was a Bronze Age urban civilisation.
  • Distinct features [[P24 Q1a(xiii): B]]: planned towns (grid streets, citadel + lower town), fire-baked (burnt) bricks, an excellent drainage system, pictographic seals (steatite, undeciphered script), standard weights, fire-baked painted pottery, trade (Mesopotamia, Afghanistan), bullock-carts.
  • Town planning: raised citadel in the west (Great Bath, granary) and a lower town with houses; streets cut each other at right angles; standard-size bricks (1:2:4); wells in houses and streets; public Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro. [[P25 Q6b]]
  • Drainage & sanitation: every house had a bath-room and drain; house drains joined covered brick drains along the streets, with manholes and soak pits; waste bins stood outside shops/houses and were emptied. This kept the town clean and healthy — “effective town planning / waste management”. [[P24 Q1b(iii)]] [[P25 Q1e(v)]]
  • Religion: no big temples found. Evidence: terracotta mother goddess figures, the “Pashupati” seal (a seated, yoga-like figure surrounded by animals), worship of trees (pipal), animals (bull) and water (Great Bath for ritual bathing). So “worshipping in church” is wrong. [[P24 Q1e(i)]]

Solved questions

“The religious life of the Indus Valley shares similar features with Buddhist practices in Bhutan.” Do you agree? @@Yes.@@ The Indus people worshipped nature — trees, animals and water — and the “Pashupati” seal shows a meditating (yogic) posture; Bhutanese Buddhism also practises meditation and respect for sacred trees, rivers and animals. (One reason is enough.) FOUR features of the Indus Valley Civilisation evident in my community:
  1. Planned town / roads: Thimphu’s organised streets and zones.
  2. Drainage & waste management: covered drains, manholes and waste bins outside shops.
  3. Strong baked/durable buildings: houses of bricks, cement and stone.
  4. Trade & markets: weekend markets, weights and measures, barter/seal-like stamps on goods; also granaries/storage (community storehouses, dzong granaries).
 Effectiveness of the drainage system and TWO solutions: Effective because covered drains stopped flooding, sewage and disease, and house drains were linked to street drains with manholes for cleaning. Solutions for my community: (1) build covered drains with manholes and separate storm-water from waste-water; (2) community waste bins + regular cleaning / rainwater harvesting and soak pits so no waste enters the drains. FIVE aspects of Indus town planning for modern cities:
  1. Grid-pattern roads for easy movement.
  2. Covered drainage with manholes.
  3. Zoning — separate administrative/religious area and residential area.
  4. Standard durable bricks / strong buildings and building rules.
  5. Public water supply, public baths, waste bins and community granaries (planned services).
Sec A / blanks: P24 xiii B (fire-baked pottery, drainage pattern, pictographic seals). P24 b(iii) “…Indus Valley’s effective town planning / sanitation”.

2 · Vedic Religion & Buddhism
P24 Q1a(v,xv),Q4a,Q5a · P25 Q1a(xiv),Q1e(iv),Q7b,c

Vedic religion (c. 1500–600 BCE)

  • Based on the Vedas (Rig Veda oldest). Gods of nature: Indra, Agni, Varuna, Surya. Worship by yajna (fire sacrifice) with chanting by Brahmin priests.
  • Animal sacrifice (cows, horses — e.g. Ashvamedha) was part of the rituals; society divided into four varnas (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) which became a rigid caste system.
  • Why Buddhism became prominent [[P24 Q1a(xv): C]]: in the 6th century BCE India was in the midst of religious and philosophical reform — costly rituals, animal sacrifice, Brahmin dominance and caste discrimination made people look for simpler, more equal paths (Buddhism, Jainism, Upanishadic ideas). The new cities and traders supported them.

The Buddha

  • Siddhartha Gautama, Shakya prince, born c. 563 BCE at Lumbini (Kapilavastu); father Suddhodana, mother Maya. Married Yashodhara; son Rahula. Buddha = “Awakened One” (from budh, to awaken) [[P24 Q1a(v): B]] (check: D “Enlightened One” is also used).
  • Four Sights: an old man, a sick man, a dead man, a wandering ascetic → Great Renunciation at 29: he left his palace to seek truth and an end to suffering. [[P25 Q1a(xiv): D]]
  • Six years of extreme fasting nearly killed him → he accepted food (Sujata’s rice-milk) and chose the Middle Path between luxury and self-torture. [[P25 Q1e(iv)]]
  • Enlightenment at Bodh Gaya under the Bodhi tree (age 35) → First Sermon at Deer Park, Sarnath to five ascetics → taught for 45 years → Mahaparinirvana at Kushinagar (age 80).
  • Four Noble Truths: (1) life has suffering (dukkha); (2) its cause is craving; (3) suffering can end (nirvana); (4) the way is the Eightfold Path.
  • Five precepts: no killing, stealing, false speech, sexual misconduct or intoxicants. Right Action = not killing/harming, not stealing, no sexual misconduct.

Solved questions

“Siddhartha denounced extreme asceticism in favour of the Middle Path.” Justify with TWO reasons: (1) after six years of severe fasting his body became so weak he nearly died and he saw that starving the body does not bring wisdom; (2) he realised both luxury (palace life) and self-torture are extremes that fail to end suffering — so he accepted food, regained strength and attained Enlightenment through balance and meditation. Illustrate TWO principles I practise daily: (1) Kindness / compassion — I share my lunch with a classmate who has none and look after stray dogs; (2) Equality — I treat all classmates, rich or poor, boys or girls, with the same respect and do not tease anyone. (Humanity: helping a sick neighbour or an old person.) THREE ways to live in harmony through Right Action: (1) do not harm or kill any living being — solve disputes peacefully; (2) do not steal or cheat — respect others’ property and be honest; (3) act with care in relationships and help others through community service (volunteering, sharing), avoiding sexual misconduct/intoxicants that hurt others. “Animal sacrifice was essential in Vedic religion. Was it a good practice?” @@No@@ — (1) it causes cruelty and suffering to innocent animals; (2) it wasted valuable cattle needed for farming and milk, harming the economy; (3) it made rituals costly, so poor people were excluded; (4) it raised the priests’ power and caste inequality; (5) it was against non-violence and led to protest movements — Buddhism and Jainism taught ahimsa. As prayer captain, FIVE activities to enhance spiritual life in my school: (1) lead morning and evening prayers and the Zhabdrung / Kuenrig prayer; (2) invite a lama or teacher for a Dharma talk; (3) organise meditation sessions; (4) arrange butter-lamp and tshogs offerings on auspicious days (e.g. Lhabab Duchen); (5) clean the lhakhang and plan community-service / animal-release (tshethar) activities. (Also: kora to a nearby temple, choeku, reading scriptures.)
Quick: P24 v B; xv C. P25 xiv D Great Renunciation (not First Sermon = Sarnath; Enlightenment = Bodh Gaya; Mahaparinirvana = death).

Ashoka & the spread of Buddhism

  • After the bloody Kalinga war (261 BCE) Emperor Ashoka turned to Buddhism and non-violence (dhamma), carved edicts on rocks and pillars and sent missionaries abroad (including to Sri Lanka).
  • Buddhism travelled along trade routes to Central Asia, China, Tibet and South-East Asia; later the Vajrayana form reached Bhutan.

  • Why did people accept Buddhism? simple teachings in local language, no costly rituals, equality for all castes, non-violence.
3 · The Age of Exploration
P24 Q1a(vi,vii),b(vii),d(iv) · P25 Q1a(xv),c(i,v)

Key ideas

  • When & who: 15th–17th centuries; pioneers were Portugal and Spain (later Holland, England, France).
  • Causes (the 3 Gs): God (spread Christianity), Gold (wealth, spices, trade) and Glory (power and fame). The Ottoman capture of Constantinople (1453) blocked the old land routes, so Europe searched for sea routes to Asia. [[P24 Q1b(vii)]] (“…as well as for spreading Christianity”).
  • Equipment used by explorers: map, ship (caravel), compass, astrolabe. The printing press was NOT sailors’ equipment. [[P24 Q1a(vii): D]]
  • Voyages: Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope (1488); Columbus (Spain) reached the Americas in 1492; Vasco da Gama reached Calicut, India, by sea in 1498; Magellan’s fleet sailed round the world (1519–22).
  • Effects — positive: globalisation: economic and cultural links [[P25 Q1a(xv): D]]; the Columbian Exchange spread crops (potato, maize, tomato), animals, ideas, technology and goods [[P25 Q1c(v): d. Columbus]]; better maps and knowledge. Negative: conquest and colonisation, slave trade, diseases (smallpox) that killed millions of native people, loss of native cultures.
  • Triangular trade: Europe → West Africa carrying manufactured goods (guns, cloth, iron); Africa → the Americas carrying enslaved Africans (Middle Passage); Americas → Europe carrying raw materials (sugar, cotton, tobacco). [[P24 Q1a(vi)]] [[IMG triangular trade map]]
  • Maritime trade conflict: reasons include trade-route control and a growing demand for marine resources (fish, oil, minerals) → TRUE [[P24 Q1d(iv)]].

4 · Colonialism & Indian Nationalism
P24 Q3c · P25 Q7a

Key ideas

  • British rise: East India Company (1600) → factories (Surat 1612, Madras, Bombay, Calcutta) → Plassey 1757 and Buxar 1764 → the Diwani (right to collect revenue of Bengal, 1765) → subsidiary alliances and the Doctrine of Lapse → Revolt of 1857 → Crown rule 1858.
  • Why the Mughals weakened: Aurangzeb’s death (1707), wars of succession, weak emperors, revolts of Marathas, Rajputs, Nawabs; Nadir Shah’s invasion (1739). The emperor’s 1717 farman gave the Company duty-free trade in Bengal.
  • Colonial rule: heavy land tax, ruin of handicrafts (de-industrialisation), famines, racial discrimination, Indians kept out of high posts.
  • Rise of nationalism: English education and Western ideas (liberty, equality); newspapers; reformers (Ram Mohan Roy, Arya Samaj); railways and telegraph linking the country; unpopular acts (Partition of Bengal 1905, Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh 1919). Indian National Congress founded 1885; Gandhi’s non-violent movements; independence 15 Aug 1947.
[[fig:colonial|Indian history under the British: key dates.]]

Solved questions

“Colonialism was the main reason behind the rise of Indian nationalism.” Do you agree? @@Yes, largely.@@ (1) Economic exploitation — high taxes, destruction of handicrafts and famines united people against the British. (2) Political domination and racial discrimination — Indians were denied high posts and rights, so they demanded self-rule. (3) Western education and the press (a colonial creation) taught ideas of freedom and let leaders connect; the Congress was formed in 1885. (4) Oppressive laws (Partition of Bengal, Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh) turned moderate opinion into mass movements. (Other causes, e.g. social reform, helped but the British rule created the common enemy.) As a Mughal Emperor, FIVE measures to stop British colonisation: (1) cancel the duty-free trading farman and tax British goods like other traders; (2) allow no forts or private armies in Company factories and keep ports under Mughal officers; (3) unite the Rajputs, Marathas and Nawabs and end succession wars so the empire speaks with one voice; (4) modernise the army with muskets, artillery and a navy, and pay soldiers regularly; (5) play European powers (French, Dutch, Portuguese) against the British and gather intelligence on their plans.
