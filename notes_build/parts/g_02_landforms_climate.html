3 · Landforms: Wind, Rivers & Underground Water (Karst)
P25 Q1d,Q7a,b · P23 Q1a(vi),Q6a,b,Q7e

Agents of gradation

  • Erosion (wearing away) → transportation (carrying) → deposition (dropping when energy is lost). Agents: running water, wind, glaciers, underground water, waves.
  • Deposition happens when the river loses energy and drops the material it was carrying [[P25 Q1d(ii): a]] — e.g. delta, floodplain, alluvial fan.

Work of wind (arid areas)

  • Erosion: deflation = wind lifts and blows away loose dust and sand [[P25 Q1d: f]]; abrasion = wind-carried particles strike and wear the rock surface (sand-blasting) [[P25 Q1d(iv): e]].
  • Transportation [[P25 Q1d]]: suspension — fine soil particles lifted and held in the air (v: b); saltation — grains bounce along the ground up to about 1.5 m (iii: c); surface creep — larger, heavy particles pushed and rolled along the ground (i: d).
  • Erosional features: mushroom (pedestal) rocks, yardangs and zeugen (ridges), deflation hollows/ oases, desert pavement.
  • Depositional features: sand dunes (barchan = crescent-shaped; seif = long), loess (fine wind-blown silt, fertile).

Work of underground water — Karst

  • Karst topography = landscape of limestone (soluble rock) shaped by the dissolving action of underground water, with sinkholes, caves and underground drainage. [[P23 Q7e]]
  • Process: rainwater absorbs CO₂ → weak carbonic acid → dissolves limestone (calcium carbonate) along joints (solution).
  • Erosional features: lapies (grooved ridges), sinkholes/dolines (funnel-shaped hollows), swallow holes (where streams vanish underground), caves/caverns [[P23 Q1a(vi): B stalactite is NOT erosional]].
  • Depositional features (dripping water leaves calcite): stalactites (hang from roof), stalagmites (grow up from floor), pillars/columns (when they join). [[P23 Q6a]]
[[fig:karst|Karst: limestone cave with erosional and depositional features.]]

Solved questions

How a stalagmite forms: water containing dissolved calcium bicarbonate drips from the cave roof; when a drop hits the floor it loses CO₂ and evaporates, leaving a small deposit of calcite. Drop after drop, the deposit builds upward into a cone. (If it meets a stalactite growing down, they form a pillar.) TWO negative impacts of tourism on karst landscapes: (1) tourists touch, break or write on stalactites and stalagmites (they grow only ~1 cm in 100 years), and trample the surface; (2) waste, litter, oil and sewage pollute the underground water and cave, and lights/crowds disturb bats and cave life. (i) Figure 1 shows a pillar (column) in a limestone cave. (ii) It forms when a stalactite growing down from the roof and a stalagmite growing up from the floor join, by repeated deposition of calcite from dripping water. TWO features formed by wind in arid areas: (1) Mushroom rock – wind-blown sand abrades the base of a rock more than the top, giving a narrow stem and wide cap (erosion); (2) Sand dunes – wind drops sand where it loses speed, forming crescent-shaped barchans or long seif dunes (deposition). (Others: yardangs, loess, deflation hollows.) Karst topography: the unusual landscape that develops on limestone when slightly acidic rainwater and underground water dissolve the rock, creating sinkholes, caves, underground streams and, where water drips, stalactites and stalagmites.

4 · Climate, Vegetation & Houses of Bhutan
P25 Q1a(ii,v),c(ii,iii),Q2e,Q7d · P23 Q1a(iii),Q3b,Q5c,d,Q7d

Factors controlling Bhutan’s climate

  • Altitude (the main factor) — Bhutan rises from ~100 m to 7,500 m; temperature falls ~6.5 °C per 1000 m. High regions are cooler [[P25 Q1c(ii): lower]]. Gasa has cool summers and cold dry winters mainly because of its altitude [[P25 Q1a(v): C]].
  • Mountains (direction/orientation of ranges): they block the monsoon winds — windward southern slopes get heavy rain (5000+ mm), leeward inner valleys and the north are dry (rain shadow); mountains force air to rise and cool, forming precipitation (orographic). Mountain ranges affect precipitation → 2025 b(iv) TRUE.
  • Monsoon: moist south-west monsoon winds from the Bay of Bengal bring most rain in June–September. The graph’s rainfall rise from January to May shows the approach of the summer monsoon [[P25 Q1a(ii): B]].
  • Latitude (26.7°–28.3° N, sub-tropical) gives high sun angle, but altitude overrides it.
  • Seasons: winter (Dec–Feb), spring (Mar–May), summer monsoon (Jun–Sep), autumn (Oct–Nov).

Solved questions

“The Himalayan ranges affect Bhutan’s climate.” @@Agree.@@ (1) They act as a barrier to the monsoon: the southern slopes receive heavy rain while the inner valleys are in a rain shadow and dry. (2) Their high altitude makes the north very cold and they shield Bhutan from freezing Siberian winds; the north–south valleys also create different local climates. Figure A is alpine vegetation, Figure B is coniferous forest: (1) A = treeless grassland/shrubs; B = dense tall conifers. (2) A grows at very high altitude with very cold climate; B grows at a lower altitude with a cool, moist climate. Thick-walled houses with sloping roofs (Lunana): (1) thick stone or rammed-earth walls keep heat inside and block the freezing wind; (2) sloped roofs of wood/slate let snow and rain slide off so the roof does not collapse and does not leak; (also: local, light, easy-to-repair materials). Design for a very cold place: thick insulated walls, small south-facing windows, a sloping roof, a tight door, low ceilings and animals kept below the living floor. Highlander (cordyceps) or southern farmer? Sample answer: I would live in the south and farm. (1) The sub-tropical climate and fertile land allow farming for most of the year with regular income; (2) the south has roads, markets, schools and hospitals, whereas cordyceps income is seasonal, risky and lives in a harsh climate at high altitude. (Either choice is accepted if the two reasons are sound.)

5 · Latitude, Longitude & Time
P25 Q1a(iv),Q3e,Q4a,c · P23 Q1a(ii),c(i),Q3a,Q5b,Q7c

Key ideas

  • Latitude = angular distance north/south of the Equator (0°–90°); parallels. Longitude = angular distance east/west of the Prime Meridian (0°, Greenwich); meridians (0°–180°).
  • Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours → 15° = 1 hour → 1° = 4 minutes. East is ahead; west is behind.
  • Standard time: Bhutan 90° E → GMT + 6 h; India 82½° E → GMT + 5½ h; London (GMT) 0°; Singapore 104° E; Sydney 151° E.
  • Uses of latitude [[P25 Q4a]]: (1) locates a place and its climate zone (tropical, temperate, polar); (2) gives day length and seasons; (3) used with longitude for navigation; (4) measures north–south distance (1° ≈ 111 km).
  • GMT = mean solar time at Greenwich. Limitations [[P25 Q4c]]: based on one meridian only and the Sun, so it varies by place and season; Earth’s rotation is not perfectly steady, so it cannot meet the needs of atomic-precision timekeeping (GPS, internet, aviation), which now use UTC; it ignores daylight-saving changes and large countries using several zones.
  • GPS (Global Positioning System) is an advanced method of finding latitude and longitude using satellites → 2023 c(i) FALSE.
[[fig:timezones|Time and longitude.]]

Solving time problems

  1. Find the longitude difference (subtract if both are E or both W; add if one is E and one W).
  2. Multiply by 4 minutes (or divide by 15 for hours).
  3. Add the time difference for a place to the east; subtract for a place to the west.

Solved questions

9 AM in Bhutan → London: difference 6 h (Bhutan is ahead), so 9 − 6 = 3 AM (C). Sydney 151° E, Bhutan 90° E: difference = 61° × 4 = 244 min = 4 h 04 min; Sydney is east (ahead): 5:00 a.m. + 4 h 04 = 9:04 a.m. (B). Singapore time 5:48 PM, Delhi (77° E) 4:00 PM → Singapore is ahead by 1 h 48 min = 108 min. Longitude difference = 108 ÷ 4 = 27°. Singapore is east: 77° + 27° = 104° E. Delhi (70° E) 8 p.m.; Place A 5 a.m. (next day) = 9 hours ahead → Place A is east. Difference = 9 × 15 = 135°. Longitude of A = 70° + 135° = 205° E = 155° W. X is 20° W, Y is 50° W. X (20° W) is farther east, so it sees the sunrise first. Difference = 50° − 20° = 30° × 4 = 120 min = 2 hours (Y has sunrise 2 hours later). Changlimithang 6 p.m. (90° E) → London (0°): 90° × 4 = 360 min = 6 h behind → 12 noon (GMT) on 19 August 2022. (In August London uses British Summer Time, GMT + 1, so broadcasts appear at 1 p.m.; the standard textbook answer is 12 noon.) GMT’s limits: (1) it is tied to the Sun at one meridian and Earth’s rotation is irregular, so it is not precise enough for modern needs (satellites, banking, aviation) which use UTC from atomic clocks; (2) it does not suit countries spanning many longitudes or those that change clocks for daylight saving, causing confusion across time zones. TWO uses of latitude: (1) it tells which climatic zone a place lies in (hot tropics, temperate, cold polar) and the length of day and seasons; (2) it is used to locate places and navigate (ships, aircraft) and to measure north–south distance.
