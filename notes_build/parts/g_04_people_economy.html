8 · Population & Settlements
P25 Q1a(x),Q2b,Q3a,c,Q4b,e,Q6c · P23 Q1a(viii,ix),c(iii,v),Q3d,Q4a,b,Q7a

Key ideas

  • Population change = births − deaths + (immigrants − emigrants). Growth rate = % change per year. Bhutan’s rate fell from 1.09% (2012) to 0.64% (2022); the world’s rate from 1.19% (2015) to 0.91% (2024). Projected Bhutan population: 727,100 (2017) → 797,300 (2027) → 883,900 (2047).
  • Population projection lets planners estimate how demographics will develop (schools, jobs, housing) → 2023 c(iii) TRUE.
  • Life expectancy (70.2 years in 2017) rose through better healthcare, education and living standards/clean water [[P25 Q1a(x): D = i, ii, iv]]. As it rises, the mortality (death) rate decreases [[P23 Q1a(ix): B]].
  • Distribution: uneven — dense in the west (Thimphu, Paro, Chukha) and southern foothills; sparse in the high north and east. Causes: relief, climate, roads, jobs and services.
  • Migration: internal (within the country, e.g. village to village in the same dzongkhag, rural → urban) [[P23 Q1c(v): TRUE]]; international (to other countries). Push factors (poverty, no jobs, wildlife damage) and pull factors (jobs, services). Gungtong = households left empty when families move out of villages.
  • Central Place Theory (Walter Christaller, 1933): a town is a central place serving its surrounding area; low-order goods (salt, rice) are bought at the nearest small place so people travel short distances for them, high-order goods (hospital, university, car) are bought at larger towns, so people travel farther. Hexagonal market areas. Threshold (minimum demand) and range (maximum distance people travel).
  • Incorrect statement about CPT: “the number of larger towns and smaller towns is roughly equal” — in fact smaller towns are many, larger towns few [[P23 Q1a(viii): D]]. Correct: same-size towns evenly spaced; removing a town readjusts the system; larger towns are spaced farther apart.

Solved questions

Growth 2022 → 2027 = 797.3 − 763.2 = 34.1 thousand (34,100 people); as a percentage: 34.1 ÷ 763.2 × 100 = 4.47 % in five years. TWO factors for the fall in world population growth: (1) lower birth rates from family planning/contraception and women’s education and employment; (2) urbanisation and higher cost of living, so couples marry later and have fewer children (also fewer child deaths, so parents need fewer births). Central Place Theory: people prefer to spend less time and money on travel, so for cheap daily necessities such as salt and rice (low-order goods, bought often) they go to the nearest small village shop/market; specialised goods and services (hospital, college) are found only in larger towns, which are fewer and farther apart. So small centres are many and close, big ones few and far. As Prime Minister, to settle people in sparsely populated areas: (1) decentralise services and jobs — build schools, hospitals, roads, electricity and internet, and set up agro- and cottage industries in these areas; (2) give incentives: land, housing, tax benefits, higher wages and loans for people who settle there. 42,829 Bhutanese abroad: wise, if planned. (1) They get better jobs and income and send remittances, which support families and the economy; (2) they gain skills and experience that can be brought back. (Against: brain drain and labour shortage — so the policy should be to return with skills.) If the growth rate keeps falling: (1) an ageing population — more old people and a higher dependency ratio, more pressure on health and pensions; (2) a shrinking workforce and labour shortage, fewer students (school closures) and slower economic growth, so Bhutan may need immigrant workers. To reduce Gungtong, besides services and wages: (1) create employment in the village — agro-processing, cottage industries, ecotourism and homestays, farm cooperatives; (2) commercialise farming: market access, machinery, irrigation, protection from wildlife and crop insurance; also awareness and rural credit/youth farm schemes. International immigrants to fill the labour shortage: Agree, in a controlled way. (1) They supply workers in construction, farming and services and keep the economy growing; (2) they bring skills and investment. (Concern: culture, security and population limits → regulated by permits.) (Disagree is also accepted with two reasons.) TWO criticisms of CPT: (1) it assumes an unrealistic flat, uniform plain with the same resources and population everywhere — mountains, rivers and uneven wealth upset the pattern; (2) it ignores transport and modern shopping (roads, vehicles, online shopping), and people do not always choose the nearest centre. If Thimphu’s population keeps growing (150,595 by 2027): (1) housing shortage, high rents and squatter settlement; (2) traffic congestion, pollution and pressure on water, electricity and waste services; also unemployment and crime.

9 · Agriculture, Land & Food Security
P25 Q2e,Q3b,Q4d,Q5e · P23 Q1a(vii),d(v),Q3c,Q6d

Key ideas

  • Subsistence farming = small-scale farming for the family’s own food using primitive tools and human/animal labour (the past type in Bhutan) [[P23 Q1d(v): subsistence]]. Commercial farming = large-scale, machinery, hybrid seeds, for sale.
  • Land fragmentation = farm land divided into small scattered plots as it is shared among children. Best method: farm cooperatives (pool land and work together) [[P23 Q1a(vii): A]]; also land consolidation.
  • Food self-sufficiency: Bhutan still imports rice, edible oil and vegetables. Challenges: shortage of farm labour (rural–urban migration), human–wildlife conflict, small fragmented steep fields, limited irrigation, climate change, pests. Ways: farm mechanisation, electric fencing, irrigation, cooperatives, markets, climate-smart methods, youth in agriculture.
  • Pests: fall armyworm (maize, since 2019) and Giant African Land Snail (vegetables, since 2010). Strategies: Integrated Pest Management: early monitoring and reporting, pheromone/light traps, hand-picking and destroying, biological control (natural enemies, neem), crop rotation/early planting, quarantine and farmer training.
  • Climate-smart agriculture (CSA): seed saving, crop rotation, terracing, water harvesting, agroforestry. It raises productivity by keeping soil fertile, saving water and soil and reducing risk from weather.

Solved questions

Punakha farmers face challenges adopting modern technology: (1) fields are small, terraced and fragmented, so machines such as power tillers and harvesters cannot be used easily; (2) shortage of labour, money, training and extension support (and crop damage by wild animals) make farmers hesitate to take risks with new methods. Strategies against fall armyworm and snails: (1) monitor fields early and control biologically/with traps (pheromone traps, neem sprays, natural enemies), hand-pick and destroy snails; (2) awareness and training for farmers in integrated pest management, crop rotation and quarantine so pests do not spread. Does climate-smart agriculture increase productivity? @@Yes.@@ (1) Crop rotation and seed saving keep soil fertile, cut pests and give locally adapted seed at low cost; (2) terracing stops soil erosion and holds water on steep slopes so yields stay stable under changing climate. The picture shows manual paddy transplanting (traditional farming). Drawbacks: (1) it is labour-intensive, slow and tiring (bending for hours) and needs many workers; (2) yields and income are low because of small fields and primitive tools, so young people leave farming. Bhutan is not self-sufficient in rice, oil and vegetables. TWO challenges and solutions: (1) shortage of labour as people migrate → introduce machinery, labour-saving tools and cooperatives; (2) crop damage by wild animals/pests and small fragmented holdings → electric fences, IPM, land pooling and irrigation.

Solved questions

FOUR negative impacts of mining: (1) deforestation and habitat loss; (2) air pollution (dust, blasting fumes) and noise; (3) water pollution and siltation of rivers by waste; (4) soil erosion, landslides and scarred land; also health problems of workers and nearby people. FOUR positive influences of technology on industries: (1) higher production and productivity (machines work faster); (2) better quality and lower cost; (3) safer work and less labour needed in dangerous tasks; (4) new products, new jobs and wider markets (online sales); also less waste and energy saved. Is it profitable for Bhutan to set up many mining industries? @@No, not many.@@ (1) Deposits are small and scattered in steep terrain, so mining and transport costs are high; (2) the damage to forests, rivers and tourism-based biodiversity costs more than the profit and goes against the pledge to protect nature (60% forest cover). “Mining gives huge benefits at the cost of the environment.” @@Agree.@@ (1) Mining gives revenue, jobs and raw material for industries (cement, steel); (2) but it destroys forests, pollutes air and water, causes landslides and erosion and the minerals are non-renewable. How a smart factory reduces waste and improves resource use: (1) sensors and AI monitor materials, energy and machines in real time, so exact amounts are used and over-production is avoided; (2) predictive maintenance and automatic quality checks reduce breakdowns and defective products; waste is recycled back into production. TWO most important factors before setting up an industry: (1) availability of raw materials, power and water; (2) market and transport (nearness to customers, roads and railways). Also labour, capital and government policy. Long-term impacts of forest-based industries on forest ecosystems: (1) over-harvesting causes deforestation, loss of habitat and biodiversity and weak regeneration; (2) soil erosion, landslides, loss of water sources and carbon storage, which disturb the whole ecosystem (unless felling is sustainable and replanted).
