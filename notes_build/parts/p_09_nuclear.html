Chapter 9: Radioactivity & Nuclear EnergyA decay equation and an alpha/beta/gamma question appear every year

9.1 Must-know summary

TermMeaning / rule
Nucleus notationAZX: A = mass (nucleon) number = protons + neutrons; Z = atomic (proton) number. Isotopes have the same Z but different A.
RadioactivityThe spontaneous emission of radiation (α, β, γ) from unstable nuclei, which lose particles/energy to become stable (they do not "receive" extra protons or neutrons).
Alpha (α)Helium nucleus 42He (2 protons + 2 neutrons), charge +2, heavy. Highest ionising power, lowest penetration (stopped by paper/skin). Bent towards the negative plate.
Beta (β)A fast electron 0−1e (from a neutron changing to a proton), charge −1, light. Medium ionising and penetration (stopped by a few mm of aluminium). Bent strongly towards the positive plate.
Gamma (γ)High-energy EM wave, no charge or mass. Lowest ionising power, highest penetration (reduced only by thick lead/concrete). Not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.
Decay equationsAlpha: A decreases by 4, Z by 2. Beta: A unchanged, Z increases by 1. Gamma: A and Z unchanged. Totals of A and of Z must balance on both sides.
UsesTreating cancer (gamma), sterilising equipment, medical tracers (iodine-123 for the thyroid: gamma passes out through the body and has low ionising power), smoke detectors, tracers in industry.
Hazards & safetyIonising radiation damages cells (burns, cancer, mutations). Precautions: handle with long tongs, store in lead-lined containers, wear protective clothing and a radiation badge (dosimeter), keep a distance and minimise the exposure time, no eating or drinking in the area, proper disposal of waste.
Nuclear energyEnergy released by nuclear fission (heavy nuclei such as uranium split, in reactors) or fusion (light nuclei join, in stars). It is not the same as radioactivity, which is the spontaneous decay of unstable nuclei (uncontrolled and not used to generate power directly).

Mass number: S (222) is 4 less than P (226). 2023 Q5(b) || 11 % of the world's electricity is nuclear. Should Bhutan generate nuclear energy? || No. Bhutan already has abundant, clean and cheap hydropower. A nuclear plant needs huge investment and advanced technology, imported uranium, safe disposal of radioactive waste for thousands of years, and carries the risk of accidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima), especially in an earthquake-prone Himalayan country with a small grid. 2023 Q6(e)(i) || Diagram: a source in a lead box; rays deflected between + and − plates. Name radiations P and R. || P bends towards the positive plate, so it is negative: beta (β). R bends towards the negative plate: alpha (α). (Q is undeflected: gamma.) 2023 Q6(e)(ii) || Why is ray Q not deflected? || Q is gamma radiation, an electromagnetic wave with no charge, so electric and magnetic fields do not deflect it. 2023 Q8(f) || Should schools use radioactive materials in laboratories? || No, not without strict control. Radiation ionises and damages living cells (burns, cancer, mutations); students may mishandle, lose or be exposed to the source, and storage and disposal need special facilities. Safer ways: simulations, videos and demonstrations by trained experts using very weak sealed sources. 2024 Q5(d) || TWO safety precautions in a nuclear power plant. || (1) Shield the reactor with thick concrete and lead and use remote handling; (2) wear protective suits with radiation badges, limit the exposure time and monitor radiation levels; also safe storage of waste and regular training. 2024 Q8(b) || Which characteristic (mass or charge) determines the ionising power and which the penetration depth of α, β, γ? || Charge determines the ionising power (alpha +2 is highest, beta −1 medium, gamma 0 lowest). Mass determines the penetration (heavy alpha is stopped by paper, light beta by aluminium, massless gamma penetrates most). 2024 Q9(a) || 147N + 42α → X + 11H. Proton number and nucleon number of X? || Nucleon number: 14 + 4 = 18 = X + 1 ⇒ 17. Proton number: 7 + 2 = 9 = X + 1 ⇒ 8. X is 178O (oxygen-17). 2025 Q4(d) || U-238 (Z = 92) alpha decays to thorium, then thorium beta decays to protactinium. Write the equations. || 23892U → 23490Th + 42He and 23490Th → 23491Pa + 0−1e (Fig 9.2). 2025 Q5(e) || Is nuclear energy the same as radioactivity? || No. Radioactivity is the spontaneous decay of unstable nuclei emitting α, β or γ (it cannot be controlled). Nuclear energy is the energy released in nuclear reactions (fission or fusion), controlled in reactors to produce electricity. A reactor uses radioactive material (uranium) but the two ideas are different. 2025 Q6(f) || The Japanese PM ate fish from Fukushima after treated water was released. What conclusions about the effects of nuclear radiation on living things? || Radiation is harmful in large doses (it damages cells, causes cancer and mutations, and radioactive substances can build up in the food chain), which is why the water was treated and monitored. The PM's action suggests that when the radioactivity is reduced below the safety limit, the harm is negligible: the effect depends on the dose and the type of radiation, so continuous monitoring is needed.

Chapter 10: Space Exploration & the UniverseAn "explain / compare / justify" question comes from this chapter every year

10.1 Must-know summary

TopicKey points
Orbiter spacecraftEnters and stays in orbit around a planet or moon; studies it for a long time, maps it and sends data (needs fuel to slow down and be captured). E.g. Mars orbiters, Chandrayaan orbiter.
Flyby missionPasses a planet once without orbiting or landing, collecting data briefly; needs little fuel, can visit several bodies. E.g. Voyager 1 (Jupiter, Saturn), New Horizons.
Rover / landerLands and moves on the surface, collects soil and rock samples and searches for signs of life (Perseverance, Curiosity). Cheaper and safer than humans, but slow and limited.
Human space explorationAstronauts travel (Apollo 11, 1969): flexible and can make decisions and bring samples back, but very expensive and risky to life. Its main impact: technological advances with wide uses on Earth (satellites, medical devices, materials).
Robotic explorationUse of autonomous or remote-controlled spacecraft to study celestial bodies.
Pros and consAdvantages: more knowledge of the universe, minerals and resources, new technology, inspires young scientists. Disadvantages: very expensive (economy diverted, possibly leading to poverty), risk to astronauts, space debris.
Life on other planetsNeeds liquid water, suitable temperature, an atmosphere and an energy source (conditions we know from Earth). Habitable-zone planets, water on Mars and Europa suggest possibility, but no life has been found yet.
Big Bang theoryThe universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding. Evidence: galaxies moving apart (red shift) and the cosmic microwave background. Critics point out that it does not explain what existed before or caused the bang, the singularity cannot be tested, and observations such as very old structures or dark matter/energy are not fully explained.
Red shiftLight from receding galaxies has a longer wavelength (shifted towards red): evidence that the universe is expanding. (A blue shift would mean the source is approaching.)
StarsStars shine because of nuclear fusion of light elements (hydrogen → helium) in their cores. A massive star ends as a supernova (a huge explosion that can outshine a galaxy) leaving a neutron star or black hole.
Origin of the solar systemNebular hypothesis (Fig 10.1): a cloud of gas and dust collapses, flattens into a spinning disc, forms the Sun and, by accretion, planets.

2023 Q2(f) || Many cosmologists believe in a big bang but some oppose it. Possible reasons for disagreement? || (1) The theory cannot explain what existed before the bang or what caused it; the starting point (singularity) cannot be tested by experiment. (2) Some observations (e.g. very old galaxies, dark matter and dark energy) or alternative models (such as the steady-state model) are not fully explained by it. 2023 Q4(f) || Differentiate orbiter and flyby space exploration. || Orbiter: goes into orbit around the planet and studies it over a long time; gives detailed data; needs more fuel and cost. Flyby: passes the planet only once without orbiting or landing; gets data only for a short time, but needs little fuel and can visit several planets (Voyager 1). 2023 Q7(f) || Is there a possibility of life on other planets? || Yes, it is possible. The universe has billions of galaxies and planets; some lie in the habitable zone where liquid water can exist; signs of water have been found on Mars and Europa. Life needs water, a suitable temperature, an atmosphere and energy, so such planets may support life, although none has been confirmed yet. 2024 Q5(c) || Why examine the conditions necessary for life on the Earth to study life in outer space? || The Earth is the only place where life is known to exist, so its conditions (liquid water, suitable temperature, an atmosphere, an energy source) are the reference for deciding which planets might support life. 2025 Q2(e) || Create a theoretical model for the formation of the solar system, with mechanisms and an account of the planet types. || See Fig 10.1. Model: a giant cloud of gas and dust (solar nebula) collapses under gravity (perhaps triggered by a nearby supernova) and spins faster and flattens into a disc. The centre becomes the Sun when fusion starts. Dust grains collide and stick (accretion) into planetesimals and then planets. Inner planets are small and rocky because only metals and rock could condense in the heat; outer planets are large gas/ice giants because they formed in the cold where ices and gases could gather. The model explains why the planets orbit in the same direction and plane, the asteroid belt and comets (leftover material). 2025 Q3(e) || Apollo 11 (crewed lunar landing) vs Mars 2020 Perseverance (rover): advantages and limitations. || Apollo 11: advantages — humans decide, adapt and perform complex tasks, and brought samples back; limitations — extremely costly, needs life-support, risks astronauts' lives, short stay. Perseverance rover: advantages — cheaper, no risk to human life, can work for years in harsh conditions and reach distant Mars; limitations — slow, limited instruments, signal delay so it cannot react quickly, cannot bring samples back by itself and may break down.
