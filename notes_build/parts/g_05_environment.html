11 · Biodiversity, Environment & Tourism
P25 Q1a(vii),b(v),Q6d,Q7e · P23 Q1c(ii),Q3e,Q6c,e

Key ideas

  • Biodiversity = variety of life on Earth at three levels: genetic, species and ecosystem diversity. Bhutan’s wide variety of habitats (subtropical forests to alpine meadows) is ecosystem diversity [[P25 Q1a(vii): C]]; ecosystem diversity results from species interactions and habitat variation → 2023 c(ii) TRUE.
  • Biodiversity hotspot = a region with a very large number of unique (endemic) species that is threatened by habitat loss → 2025 b(v) TRUE. Bhutan lies in the Eastern Himalaya hotspot; about 70% forest and over 50% of the land in protected areas and corridors.

  • Biodiversity and climate change [[P23 Q3e]]: forests are carbon sinks that absorb CO₂; healthy forests, wetlands and soils regulate water, reduce floods and landslides, and genetic diversity lets species adapt to changing climate.
  • Threats: deforestation, mining, roads/hydropower, vehicles and pollution, poaching, invasive species, climate change.
  • Sustainable Development Fee (SDF) for tourists (US$ 65 → 200 proposed) supports “high value, low volume” tourism.

Solved questions

A biodiversity protection plan for my community: (1) protect and plant forests — community forests, no hunting or poaching, fire protection and restricted grazing near habitats; (2) awareness and sustainable use — school clubs and village meetings, proper waste disposal, ecotourism rules, harvesting of herbs/NTFPs only in quantities that regrow. Biodiversity and climate change: (1) forests and plants absorb carbon dioxide and store carbon, slowing global warming; (2) diverse ecosystems are more resilient — they protect against floods, droughts and landslides and provide food and water; genetic diversity helps species adapt. SDF raised from US$65 to US$200: (1) fewer tourists visit, so there is less pressure, waste and disturbance in forests, trails and wildlife areas; (2) the extra money funds conservation — national parks, community projects, protection of species and carbon-neutral development. As Director of the National Environment Commission, TWO measures on the rising number of vehicles: (1) promote electric vehicles through tax and import incentives and charging stations; (2) improve public transport and limit private vehicles (quotas, emission tests, car-pooling, walking/cycling lanes).

12 · Natural Disasters & Safety
P25 Q1a(ix),c(v),Q3d,Q6b,e · P23 Q1a(x),d(ii),Q2c,Q4d,Q7b

Key ideas

  • Hazard = a threat; disaster = a hazard causing great loss of life and property. Natural: earthquake, flood, landslide, GLOF, storm, forest fire. Disaster management cycle: preparedness, response, recovery, mitigation.
  • Earthquakes: Bhutan lies in an active seismic zone because the Indian plate presses against Eurasia (e.g. 2009 Mongar earthquake). Before: secure heavy furniture, prepare a kit, practise Drop–Cover–Hold On; during: drop, take cover under a strong table and hold on, stay away from glass and walls, in the open go to open ground; after: expect aftershocks.
  • Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF): a lake dammed by moraine breaks suddenly. The Lugge Tsho GLOF of 1994 caused heavy damage in Punakha [[P25 Q1a(ix): D]]. The disaster caused by retreating glaciers due to global warming is a GLOF [[P25 Q1c(v)]]. Seismic activity is a natural trigger, not a human-made cause (human-made: global warming, deforestation, industries) [[P23 Q1a(x): B]].
  • Flash floods and landslides in the southern foothills: very heavy monsoon rain (5000+ mm), steep slopes with young, fragile Siwalik rocks and loose soil, and deforestation / road construction; short steep rivers rise very quickly.
  • Forest fires: >1,400 incidents in ten years; mostly human-caused in dry winter/spring.
  • Electric fires: unplugging appliances when not in use and safe wiring reduce fire disasters [[P23 Q1d(ii): fire]].
  • Education in Emergency (EiE): restarting teaching, play and counselling soon after a disaster.

Solved questions

TWO methods of fighting forest fires: (1) prevention and early detection — fire lines/breaks, watch towers, patrols, drones/satellite alerts, awareness and bans on open burning; (2) quick suppression — trained community fire-fighting teams with beaters and water, backed by the forest department and the use of controlled burning of fuel before dry season. Earthquake-safe buildings: (1) follow the building code: strong foundation, reinforced concrete bands/ring beams at floor and roof level, and simple symmetrical shapes; (2) use light, flexible materials (timber frames, light roofs), avoid heavy roofs and soft storeys, and retrofit old buildings; traditional dhajji / ekra walls are flexible. Technology and life-saving skills in disasters: (1) early-warning systems, phone alerts, social media and apps warn and guide people quickly; (2) GPS, drones, thermal cameras and satellite images locate victims and assess damage for rescue; also first-aid videos and online training. ONE strategy for earthquakes: Drop, Cover and Hold On during the shaking — drop to the floor, cover your head and neck under a sturdy table and hold on until it stops; before: keep an emergency kit and know safe spots and exits and fix heavy furniture to walls. Flash floods and landslides in the southern foothills: (1) very heavy monsoon rainfall quickly fills the short, steep rivers; (2) steep slopes with loose, weak Siwalik rock and soil, made worse by deforestation and road cutting, slide down easily. “Education in emergency should be carried out by every school.” @@Agree.@@ (1) It keeps learning going, so children do not lose a year of education; (2) schooling gives safety, routine and psychosocial support, helping children cope with fear and loss; (3) children are protected from abuse, child labour and early marriage, and schools can teach disaster awareness and act as shelters/centres for the community.
