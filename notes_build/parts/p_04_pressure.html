Chapter 4: Pressure, Pascal's Law & Hydraulic MachinesEvery year: at least one hydraulic calculation and several pressure MCQs

4.1 Must-know summary

TermMeaning / rule
PressureForce acting perpendicularly per unit area: P = F / A. Unit: pascal (Pa) = N/m².
Pressure due to solidsDepends on (1) the force (weight) and (2) the area of contact. P ∝ F and P ∝ 1/A. Same force on a smaller area → larger pressure (sharp knife, nail, rocket nozzle). Larger area → smaller pressure (wide bag strap, camel's feet, wide foundations, tractor tyres).
Pressure in liquidsP = ρ g h: increases with depth and density; acts in all directions; same at the same level, independent of the shape of the container. Sea water is denser than river water, so the pressure at the same depth is greater.
Atmospheric / water pressure changeAs a bubble rises, the water pressure on it falls, so it expands.
Pascal's lawPressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions through the fluid and to the walls of the container.
Hydraulic machineSmall effort force F1 on a small piston (A1) gives pressure P = F1/A1, transmitted to a large piston (A2): F2 = F1 × A2/A1. It is a force multiplier (jack, brakes, lifts, excavators). Work in = work out, so the small piston moves a longer distance.
Gas in place of liquidPascal's law holds for any fluid, including gases, but a gas is compressible: part of the effort is used in compressing it, so it is unsuitable for hydraulic machines. Liquids (oil) are used.
FormulaUse
P = F / Apressure of solids; bricks, shoes, knife, rocket nozzle
P = ρ g hhydrostatic pressure (submarine, diver)
F1/A1 = F2/A2hydraulic machines

4.3 Solved questions — Section B

2023 Q4(c) || 8 bricks (0.2 m × 0.1 m × 0.1 m, 60 N each) lie in a single layer. Minimum pressure on the ground? || Minimum pressure means the largest area: each brick lies on its 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.02 m² face. Total area = 8 × 0.02 = 0.16 m²; total weight = 8 × 60 = 480 N. P = F/A = 480 / 0.16 = 3000 Pa. 2023 Q5(c) || Hydraulic lift: piston area 0.6 m² with force 40 N lifts a 1000 N load. Area of the other piston? || F1/A1 = F2/A2 ⇒ A2 = F2 A1 / F1 = 1000 × 0.6 / 40 = 15 m². 2023 Q6(b) || TWO factors affecting the pressure due to solids. || (1) The force (weight) acting on the surface. (2) The area of contact (P = F/A). 2023 Q7(c) || Four pipes join the same tank at the same level. Is the pressure equal in all four houses? Why? || Yes. Pressure in a liquid depends only on depth, density and g (P = ρ g h), not on the length or direction of the pipes. All four pipes are at the same depth, so the pressure is equal. 2023 Q8(c) || Explain with a mathematical expression why hydraulic machines act as force multipliers. || By Pascal's law P1 = P2, so F1/A1 = F2/A2 ⇒ F2 = F1 (A2/A1). If A2 > A1, then F2 > F1: the force is multiplied by the ratio of the areas. 2024 Q2(b) || How does Pascal's principle apply to hydraulic jacks? || A small force on the small piston creates a pressure P = F1/A1 in the oil. The pressure is transmitted equally throughout the liquid and acts on the large piston, giving a large force F2 = P A2 that lifts the car. 2024 Q3(c) || Does Pascal's principle hold for a gas? Reason? || Yes: a gas is a fluid, and the pressure applied to an enclosed gas is also transmitted equally in all directions. (But a gas is compressible, so it is not used in hydraulic machines.) 2024 Q6(a) || A pump lifts a 1000 N crate; the small piston (50 cm²) applies 500 N. Area of the larger piston? || A2 = F2 A1 / F1 = 1000 × 50 / 500 = 100 cm². 2024 Q6(b)(i) || Factors contributing to the Titan submersible implosion at 3500 m. || (1) The enormous water pressure at 3500 m: P = ρ g h ≈ 1030 × 10 × 3500 ≈ 3.6 × 107 Pa (about 350 times atmospheric pressure). (2) A weakness of the hull (composite material, fatigue from repeated dives, a design/testing flaw) that could not withstand this pressure. 2024 Q6(b)(ii) || Why an implosion rather than an explosion? || The pressure outside (water) was far greater than the pressure inside (about 1 atmosphere), so the hull collapsed inwards. An explosion happens when the pressure inside is much greater than outside. 2025 Q3(b) || F1 = 306 N on a 2 m² piston. Pressure on the large piston? || P1 = F1/A1 = 306 / 2 = 153 Pa. By Pascal's law the pressure is the same everywhere, so P2 = 153 Pa (N/m²). 2025 Q5(c) || Explain the working of a hydraulic jack. || A small force on the small piston produces pressure P = F1/A1 in the liquid. By Pascal's law this pressure is transmitted undiminished to the large piston of area A2, which therefore exerts a larger force F2 = P A2 = F1A2/A1 that lifts the car (the small piston moves farther, so work in = work out). 2025 Q6(c) || Relationship between surface area and pressure (sharp knife). || For the same force, pressure is inversely proportional to the surface area (P = F/A): the sharp edge has a small area, so a large pressure cuts easily. 2025 Q8(b) || A 300 kg wooden block, 5 m × 0.5 m, lies flat. Pressure on the ground? || Weight = 300 × 10 = 3000 N; area = 5 × 0.5 = 2.5 m². P = 3000 / 2.5 = 1200 Pa (1176 Pa if g = 9.8). 2025 Q9(c) || Explain Pascal's law. || Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions throughout the fluid and to the walls of its container.

Chapter 5: Heat, Specific Heat Capacity & CalorimetryA specific-heat calculation appears every year

5.1 Must-know summary

TermMeaning / rule
Heat vs temperatureHeat is energy transferred (J); temperature shows how hot a body is and decides the direction of heat flow: from the hotter to the colder body. Two bodies can have the same temperature but different heat contents (a bucket and a cup of water).
Thermal equilibriumWhen two bodies in contact reach the same temperature there is no net heat flow.
Specific heat capacity (c)The heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg (or 1 g) of a substance by 1 °C. Water: 4200 J/kg°C (4.2 J/g°C, 1 cal/g°C) — very high.
Heat capacityHeat needed to raise the temperature of a given body by 1 °C = m c. Thicker (more massive) pan bases have a larger heat capacity.
Latent heatHeat absorbed or released during a change of state at constant temperature: latent heat of fusion (ice → water, 360–334 J/g), of vaporisation (water → steam). Boiling water stays at 100 °C until the last drop evaporates.
Principle of calorimetryWith no heat lost to the surroundings: heat lost by the hot body = heat gained by the cold body (law of conservation of energy). A calorimeter is a well-insulated vessel (Fig 5.2).
Applications of high s.h.c. of waterCoolant in car radiators, hot-water bags, fomentation, coastal climate, watering plants before frost (water releases heat slowly and latent heat when freezing).
FormulaUse
Q = m c ΔTheat gained or lost; c = Q / (m ΔT); same Q ⇒ smallest ΔT for the largest c
Q = m Lchange of state (latent heat)
m1c1(T1 − T) = m2c2(T − T2)mixing / calorimetry problems

5.3 Solved questions — Section B

2023 Q3(c) || The principle of calorimetry and the law of conservation of energy are similar. Agree? Justify. || Agree. Calorimetry states heat lost by the hot body equals heat gained by the cold body. This is the conservation of energy applied to heat: the energy is not created or destroyed, it is only transferred from one body to the other. 2023 Q5(d)(i) || Define specific heat capacity. || The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg (or 1 g) of a substance by 1 °C (unit J/kg°C). 2023 Q5(d)(ii) || Why do ice cubes cool a juice better than water at 0 °C? || Ice must absorb the latent heat of fusion to melt, in addition to the heat it gains afterwards. Cold water at 0 °C has no latent heat to absorb, so ice removes much more heat from the juice per gram. 2023 Q6(c)(i) || Heat needed to raise 10 kg of water by 1 °C. || Q = m c ΔT = 10 × 4200 × 1 = 42 000 J (= 10 000 cal). 2023 Q6(c)(ii) || 5 kg of water at 80 °C is mixed with 10 kg of cold water; the final temperature is 40 °C. Initial temperature of the cold water? || Heat lost by hot water = 5 × 4200 × (80 − 40) = 840 000 J. Heat gained by cold water = 10 × 4200 × (40 − T). Equate: 42 000 (40 − T) = 840 000 ⇒ 40 − T = 20 ⇒ T = 20 °C. 2023 Q8(d) || 1 g of ice at 0 °C absorbs 360 J to melt. Heat to bring 1000 g of ice to boiling point (c = 4.2 J/g°C)? || Melting: 1000 × 360 = 360 000 J. Heating water 0 → 100 °C: 1000 × 4.2 × 100 = 420 000 J. Total = 780 000 J (780 kJ). 2024 Q3(d) || How to modify the calorimeter for zero heat loss? || See Fig 5.2: put the inner (shiny, polished) vessel inside an outer jacket with insulating lagging (felt, cotton wool, polystyrene) leaving an air gap; cover it with an insulating lid with holes for the thermometer and stirrer; the vacuum flask (silvered double wall with vacuum) is the ideal. 2024 Q4(e) || A 0.05 kg metal piece needs 2.4 × 103 J to go from 27 to 327 °C. Specific heat capacity? || ΔT = 300 °C. c = Q / (m ΔT) = 2400 / (0.05 × 300) = 2400 / 15 = 160 J/kg°C. 2024 Q5(a) || Heat lost by A = 500 J; heat gained by B = 450 J. Was the principle of calorimetry confirmed? || No: the principle requires heat lost = heat gained. The 50 J difference was lost to the surroundings/calorimeter (the experiment was not perfectly insulated). 2024 Q6(d) || Graph of temperature rise of liquids A, B, C with time: which is the best radiator coolant? || Liquid C: for the same heating time its temperature rises least, so it has the highest specific heat capacity and can absorb the most heat from the engine for a small rise in temperature. 2024 Q8(d) || Which cools a soft drink faster: ice cubes at 0 °C or iced water at 0 °C? || Ice cubes: as they melt they absorb the latent heat of fusion from the drink in addition to ordinary heat, so they take more heat per gram than water at 0 °C. 2025 Q2(c) || A farmer watered his vegetables before the frost. Why? || Water has a high specific heat capacity, so the wet soil and water cool slowly, and when water freezes it releases latent heat; this keeps the plants' temperature near 0 °C and protects them from frost damage. 2025 Q4(b) || 8400 J heats 0.08 kg of metal (c = 1050 J/kg°C) from 30 °C. Final temperature? || Q = m c (T − 30) ⇒ 8400 = 0.08 × 1050 × (T − 30) = 84 (T − 30) ⇒ T − 30 = 100 ⇒ T = 130 °C. 2025 Q4(e) || Why does ice relieve the pain of a burn? || Heat flows from the hot burnt skin to the colder ice, so the skin temperature falls; the melting ice absorbs latent heat and the cold also numbs the nerve endings, reducing pain and swelling. 2025 Q8(c) || Why does the temperature drop after a sudden downpour in hot summer? || The cooler rain water absorbs heat from the hot ground and air, and its evaporation takes latent heat from the surroundings; the clouds also block sunlight. Together they lower the temperature. 2025 Q9(e) || How does a calorimeter apply the law of conservation of energy? || In an insulated calorimeter no heat escapes, so heat lost by the hot substance = heat gained by the cold substance and the vessel: m1c1(T1−T) = m2c2(T−T2). Energy is only transferred, never lost, which is used to find the specific heat capacity of an unknown substance.
