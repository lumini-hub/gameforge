5 · Historiography, Oral History & Culture
P24 Q1a(xi),b(ii),d(vii),Q4b · P25 Q1a(xii,xiii),b(e),d(iv),Q6a

Key ideas

  • History = study of the past. Historiography = the study of how history is written (the methods, sources and viewpoints of historians).
  • Medieval historiography: written by monks, priests and court scribes for religion or rulers; history shown as the will and deeds of God and of kings; mixed with myths, miracles and legends; focus on wars, dynasties, saints; ordinary people ignored. So it is best described as a historical account influenced by religious beliefs [[P24 Q1a(xi): B]]. In 2025 blank (e): “…recounting the will of deeds of God and kings / rulers”.
  • Modern historiography: based on reason, evidence and critical study of sources; objective; many themes (society, economy, women, environment, common people); uses archaeology, science, oral history; includes different schools: romanticist (emotion and imagination, glorifying heroes and the nation) and rationalist (reason and evidence) [[P24 Q1b(ii): reason / evidence]] [[P25 Q1d(iv): TRUE]].
  • Oral history = first-hand memories of living people about events they saw, collected by interviews (recent past). Oral tradition = stories, songs, proverbs and customs handed down by word of mouth over generations. They are related but not interchangeable → FALSE [[P24 Q1d(vii)]].
  • Humanising history: personal narratives let readers “connect emotionally with the experiences of individuals” and build empathy [[P25 Q1a(xiii): A]]. Others: inclusivity and diverse voices, cultural heritage, community building.
  • Culture: tangible = things you can touch (dzongs, Taktsang, thangkas, textiles, kera shoes); intangible = living practices (mask dances, folk songs, rituals, language, oral literature). [[P25 Q1a(xii)]] [[IMG 4 photos]] (check) I mask dance = intangible; II Taktsang = tangible; III traditional shoes = tangible; IV song/dance group = intangible → both A (I & III) and B (II & IV) pair one of each; B is slightly cleaner (an architectural monument + a performing tradition).
  • Bhutan must prioritise the promotion and preservation of culture / cultural heritage and traditions in a globalising world [[P24 Q1b(iv)]].

Solved questions

FIVE focuses of historical writing in the medieval period: (1) religion and the will of God — events explained as divine plan; (2) kings and rulers, their glory and victories; (3) wars, conquests and dynasties; (4) lives of saints and holy men (e.g. Bhutan’s namthars); (5) myths, miracles and legends written by monks and court scribes to please patrons; common people were ignored. Why historians prefer modern over medieval historiography: (1) it is rational and evidence-based, not myth; (2) it is objective, checking facts from many sources; (3) it covers all people — women, workers, minorities — not only kings; (4) it uses science and oral history (archaeology, carbon dating, interviews); (5) it studies cause and effect and offers many viewpoints, so we learn lessons accurately.
Quick: P24 xi B; b(ii) rationalist = reason / evidence; d(vii) FALSE. P25 xiii A; d(iv) TRUE.

6 · Zhabdrung & the Nation-State
P24 Q1a(iv,xiv),e(iii),Q2a,Q6a,Q7a · P25 Q1a(vii,viii),b(c,d),c(ii),d(iii),Q2a

Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal (1594–1651)

  • Born at Ralung, Tibet, head of the Drukpa Kagyu; left Tibet after a dispute with the Tsang ruler and arrived in Bhutan in 1616. He built the first dzong (Simtokha, 1629), then Punakha (1637), Wangdue and Paro, and drove back the Tibetan–Mongol invasions (1634, 1639, 1644). Called Palden Drukpa and the Architect of the Nation State because he created a unique national identity [[P24 Q1a(xiv): B]]. His death in 1651 was kept secret for decades; his preserved body (Machhen) rests at Punakha Dzong.
  • Laid the foundation of Bhutan’s emergence as a nation-state despite internal strife [[P25 Q1c(ii): g. Zhabdrung]].

The Choe-sid (Ten-Sid) dual system

  • Choe = religion; Sid = state. The Je Khenpo heads the monastic body; the Druk Desi runs the civil administration. “Ten-Sid” = the duality of spiritual and secular affairs [[P25 Q1a(viii): A]].
  • Chogi Chila Namsum = the three Poenlops: Trongsa, Dagana and Paro [[P24 Q1a(iv): C]] (not Desi/Je Khenpo, Drongyer etc.). Below them the Dzongpoens.
  • Significance [[P24 Q2a]]: balanced spiritual and secular power; kept peace and stability (until 1907); unified valleys under dzong administration; preserved Drukpa Kagyu identity; checks and balances.
[[fig:choesid|The Choe-sid system set up by the Zhabdrung.]] [[photo:zhabdrung|Portrait of Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal, unifier of Bhutan.]]

Laws, etiquette & identity

  • Kathrim (Zhabdrung’s law code): fundamentals Lay-Gyudrey (law of cause and effect/karma), Tha-Damtsig (sacred bond of loyalty) and the ten virtuous acts. They still resonate in modern Bhutanese society [[P25 Q1b(d)]]. Kathrim was the supreme law of the 17th century, not of modern times (the Constitution is) → FALSE [[P25 Q1d(iii)]].
  • Two laws of the 17th century: the spiritual law of the silken knot and the secular law of the golden yoke [[P25 Q1a(vii): B]]. Later the Thrimzhung Chenmo (1729) codified law.
  • Driglam Namzha = Bhutanese code of etiquette (dress, conduct, respect) promoted by the Zhabdrung [[P25 Q1b(c)]].

Solved questions

TWO significances of the Choe-sid: (1) it balanced religious and secular authority, preventing misuse of power; (2) it brought unity, peace and stable government to the country for 250 years. What if the Zhabdrung had not codified the Kathrim? (1) No common law — disputes, crime and lawlessness; (2) no unity among valleys, making Bhutan weak against Tibetan invasion; (3) no moral/ethical code (karma, loyalty) and no foundation for later laws (Thrimzhung Chenmo, Constitution) or national identity. THREE importance of Kathrim: (1) foundation of the Bhutanese legal system; (2) maintained peace, order and justice through moral principles; (3) unified people and strengthened national identity; it still guides values today. TWO factors that led the Zhabdrung to create a unique identity: (1) the need to unite warring valleys and resist Tibetan invasions; (2) a wish to establish a distinct Drukpa Kagyu Buddhist state, separate from Tibet, with its own dress, etiquette, language, dzong architecture and festivals. How the Zhabdrung promoted social cohesion through a strong national identity: (1) one religion — Drukpa Kagyu — for all; (2) Kathrim, a common law; (3) Driglam Namzha: shared dress, etiquette and conduct; (4) dzongs as common centres of administration, rituals and festivals (tshechus); (5) Choe-sid government and victory over Tibetan invasions made people feel one nation.

Solved questions

…Ugyen Wangchuck communicated with the Tibetan authorities to negotiate with the @@British@@ (Younghusband). How Ugyen Wangchuck’s leadership inspires me to resolve disputes: by staying calm, listening to both sides and using patient dialogue (not force) — as he met Colonel Younghusband in person at Chumbi. Applying his diplomacy today: (1) neutral mediation and dialogue to settle conflicts peacefully (e.g. UN talks); (2) building trust with both sides and keeping communication open, then moving step by step to a written agreement. Long-term effects: (1) strong, trusting ties with British India, raising Bhutan’s standing and protecting its independence; (2) it strengthened the Wangchuck family, leading to the monarchy in 1907 and treaties in 1910 that continued friendship. As an advisor, improve relations using the 2007 treaty autonomy: (1) open more embassies and strengthen diplomacy; (2) join international organisations (UN, BIMSTEC, SAARC); (3) widen trade and hydropower/tourism markets beyond one country; (4) cultural and GNH diplomacy — exchange programmes, scholarships; (5) keep close friendship with India while cooperating on climate and regional security. As the 16th Desi Zhidar betrayed by allies in the conflict (Cooch Behar): (1) seek peace through a respected mediator (the Panchen Lama) and negotiate a treaty; (2) rebuild unity at home — reconcile officials, test the loyalty of allies and reward the faithful; (3) strengthen defence and avoid over-extension — guard the passes and keep trade open so the British have fewer reasons to fight. Likely questions

  • Causes of the Duar War: border disputes and raids in the Duars; British wish for the fertile Duars (revenue, tea, route to Tibet); the failed Eden mission.
  • Effects of Sinchula: Bhutan lost the Duars and Dewangiri (about a fifth of its land) but gained a yearly payment and peace.
  • Why did the British support Ugyen Wangchuck? He proved a skilled mediator in 1904; he later became the stable ruler of a united Bhutan (1907).
