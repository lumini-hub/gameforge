Solved questions

(i) The donor with arrows to all groups is X = O (universal donor; no A or B antigens); the group that receives from all is Y = AB. (ii) AB is the universal recipient because its plasma has no anti-A or anti-B antibodies, so it will not clump any donor red cells. (i) RBCs lack a nucleus so there is more room for haemoglobin to carry oxygen. (ii) Group A blood has A antigen; group B plasma has anti-A antibody, which would clump the donor’s red cells. Q8b: platelets help blood to clot and seal a wound. (a) Group O blood has both anti-A and anti-B antibodies in the plasma, so it would agglutinate (clump) red cells carrying A or B antigens from A, B or AB donors; only blood without A and B antigens (group O, and matching Rh) is safe. (b) The donor must have no anti-A antibody, no B antigen and no Rh factor, because the patient has A antigen (would be attacked by anti-A), no B antigen (so has anti-B and would attack B antigen) and is Rh− (would make anti-Rh). (i) Patient = A negative (A−). (ii) Antibody A in the donated blood would attack the patient’s A-antigen red cells: agglutination, blocked vessels, possible kidney failure and death. Test tubes: no clumping with anti-A, clumping with anti-B. (i) X belongs to group B (it has B antigen only). (ii) Recipients for B blood: B and AB. (iii) Coagulation (clumping) occurred in tube B because the anti-B antibody in the reagent reacts with the B antigen on the red cells, joining them together (agglutination). A donor who can give to both A and O patients: donor O (universal donor) — but a patient of O can take only O blood, so the donor is O. P24 matching: i a plasma; ii c red blood cells.
