Households provide the factors; firms pay these incomes. All factor incomes together = national income [[P24 Q1b(v)]].

Rent

  • Rent: payment for hiring durable goods (house, land, car, furniture). In economics it extends to every factor with an inelastic (nearly fixed) supply — supply that stays the same despite a change in price [[P24 Q1c(iv): b]].
  • Factors affecting rent: (a) demand and supply of the property (more demand than supply → rent rises); (b) location (near school, hospital, town, road, market); (c) facilities (furnished, modern amenities, safety, age and type of building, cost of construction).

Investment and saving

  • Investment = creation of new capital goods (machinery, buildings, factories); also purchase of shares and bonds; spending on education and health is investment in human capital.
  • Saving = income not spent on current consumption = disposable income − consumption expenditure. Higher income and higher interest rate → more saving.
  • Why save: financial security (old age, unemployment, disability); contingencies (sickness, death, loss of property); future investment (business, education, house); interest (reward; propensity to save rises with the rate).
  • Hoarding = unspent income that earns no interest (cash in a locker or wallet); saving = unspent income that earns interest (bank deposit).
  • Too much saving reduces demand → lower profit → less production, closures and unemployment (“Know more” in the textbook).

Interest

  • Gross interest = whole payment to the lender. Net (pure) interest = gross interest − (expenses on risk + expenses on management).
  • Why borrowers pay interest: (i) lender gives up (abstains from) current consumption; (ii) risk that the borrower may not repay; (iii) money loses value over time (prices rise).
  • Simple interest SI = P × R × T|100 (on the principal only). Compound interest: A = P(1 + R|100)n, CI = A − P (“interest on interest”). Nu 1000 at 10%: SI = 100, 200, 300; CI = 100, 210, 331 (years 1, 2, 3).
  • Determination of interest: demand for capital (investment, inverse to the rate) meets supply of capital (saving, direct to the rate). Rate rises above equilibrium → excess supply → rate falls; rate falls below → excess demand → rate rises.
  • Lower interest on borrowing → more investment; higher interest on deposits → more saving [[P25 Q1c(i): d]].

Profit

  • Profit = Total revenue − Total cost (revenue = quantity × price). It is the main motive of business and moves resources to goods in higher demand (e.g. more tourists → more tourism businesses).
  • But profit should not be the only motive: business ethics (honesty, trust, responsibility, reliability, paying tax, not selling expired goods, giving back change) and social responsibility [[P24 Q1a(xviii): C]].

Solved questions — short answers and Section B

Can a ceiling on rent help housing problems? Yes, to some extent. (1) It keeps houses affordable for low- and middle-income families and stops landlords charging unfairly high rent in towns like Thimphu. (2) It protects tenants from sudden rent rises, giving security. (But a ceiling that is too low discourages owners from building or maintaining houses, so supply must also grow.) Is buying a house wise for a retired civil servant who rents in Thimphu? Yes, if he can afford it. (1) Rent keeps rising and buys no asset; a house is an asset that keeps its value and gives security and stops future rent payments. (2) Rent is a recurring cost on a fixed pension, so owning reduces monthly expenses. (Caution: it needs a large sum or loan, interest and upkeep, so he should not use up all his savings.) If borrowers do not repay: (1) banks’ funds and profit fall and non-performing loans rise, so they can lend less; (2) banks raise interest rates and become strict, so investment by businesses falls and capital formation slows; (3) business closures, job losses and slow economic growth; some banks may even fail. [3 × 1] Ways to improve saving habits: (1) awareness and financial-literacy programmes in schools and communities on the benefits of saving; (2) attractive schemes and higher interest on deposits (Piggy Bank, Child Money Back, recurring deposits) and easy banking services (mobile banking, rural branches); (also: setting saving goals, reducing wasteful spending). [2 × 1] Should a business person aim at profit only? No. (1) Business ethics and social responsibility: honesty in weights, prices and expiry dates, paying taxes; cheating customers harms trust and the business in the long run. (2) The business must respect national, social and cultural values, the environment and GNH; profit with harm to society is not sustainable. [3] Is hoarding of any benefit? Only limited benefits: (1) cash at home is ready for emergencies (illness, disasters) when a bank is far away or closed, and is safe if a bank fails; (2) gold, jewellery and stored grain keep their value and can be pledged or sold in hard times. But hoarded money earns no interest and is not available for investment, so saving in a bank is better for the economy. [2]
