Natural selection

  • Steps: (1) variation in a population; (2) more offspring are born than can survive (competition); (3) individuals with favourable traits (best adapted) survive and reproduce — fitness = ability to survive and reproduce in a given environment [[P24 Q1a(xxii): D]]; (4) they pass the advantageous alleles on, so their frequency increases over time [[P24 Q1a(xxiv): A]].
  • Example of natural selection: giraffes with longer necks reaching tall trees survive and breed → D [[P24 Q1a(xxi)]].
  • Peppered moths: soot from factories darkened trees, so dark moths were camouflaged and survived; white moths were eaten. A change in the environment can result in evolution → B [[P23 Q1a(xxiii)]].
  • Warming reduces snow, so black rabbits are better camouflaged on dark soil and thrive → C [[P25 Q1a(xiii)]]. Mice graph: brown mice increase, white mice decrease → natural selection favours brown (camouflaged in the bushy habitat) [[P23 Q5d]].
  • Sickle-cell trait is common where malaria is endemic: carriers resist malaria, so natural selection keeps the allele [[P25 Q1b(v)]].

[[fig:b_isolation|Isolation → no gene flow → differences build up → new species (speciation).]]
  • Speciation = formation of a new species. Isolated populations cannot interbreed, so mutation, selection and drift make them different until they are separate species. Mutation gives new alleles and variation, the raw material for speciation [[P25 Q3d]]; variation lets natural selection act, so allele frequencies change = evolution [[P23 Q7b(ii), Q7d(ii)]].
  • Gene flow = exchange of alleles between populations (migration) → D [[P25 Q1a(xv)]].

Genetic drift

[[fig:b_drift|Bottleneck effect and founder effect: chance changes in allele frequency in small populations.]]
  • Genetic drift = random change in allele frequency, strongest in small populations. Bottleneck effect: a storm, flood or disease kills most of a population, so genetic diversity falls [[P25 Q1b(iv)]] [[P23 Q2d]]. Founder effect: a small group colonises a new area (figure: “colonisers” leave the ancestral population) [[P24 Q8b(i)]].
  • Cheetahs have little genetic variation, so a disease or new predator can wipe out the population because no individual has a different, resistant trait [[P24 Q3b]].

Hardy–Weinberg principle

  • In a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration, selection or drift, allele frequencies stay constant from generation to generation: p + q = 1 and p2 + 2pq + q2 = 1. If p = 0.7 then q = 0.3 (q2 = 0.09, 2pq = 0.42) → A [[P24 Q1a(xxv)]].
  • It is the baseline: if frequencies change, evolution is happening and the forces responsible can be found [[P25 Q8f]]. “Only the strongest lion mates” = non-random mating/selection → does not hold the law → FALSE [[P24 Q1c(v)]].

Artificial selection & genetic engineering

  • Artificial selection (selective breeding): humans choose parents with wanted traits. Breeder picks cat 3 (low shedding) and cat 1 (calm) → A [[P24 Q1a(xxiii)]]. Hybrids (e.g. hybrid corn) combine good traits.
  • Genetic engineering (recombinant DNA) modifies DNA (nucleic acid) [[P23 Q1a(xxiv): D]] to introduce desirable traits [[P25 Q1a(xiv): D]]; introducing foreign DNA = genetic modification [[P25 Q1d(iv): b]]. It is faster and more precise than natural evolution → TRUE [[P24 Q1c(iv)]].
