Chapter 7: Poem — Good Timber (Douglas Malloch)Section C · asked in 2024 · a didactic (teaching) poem with an extended metaphor

7.1 The poem

Stanza 1
The tree that never had to fight
For sun and sky and air and light,
But stood out in the open plain
And always got its share of rain,
Never became a forest king
But lived and died a scrubby thing.

Stanza 2
The man who never had to toil
To gain and farm his patch of soil,
Who never had to win his share
Of sun and sky and light and air,
Never became a manly man
But lived and died as he began.

Stanza 3
Good timber does not grow with ease,
The stronger wind, the stronger trees,
The further sky, the greater length,
The more the storm, the more the strength.
By sun and cold, by rain and snow,
In trees and men good timbers grow.

Stanza 4
Where thickest lies the forest growth
We find the patriarchs of both.
And they hold counsel with the stars
Whose broken branches show the scars
Of many winds and much of strife.
This is the common law of life.

7.2 Must-know summary

AspectDetails
Poet & formDouglas Malloch (American poet, 1877–1938), the "lumberman's poet". Four stanzas of six lines each, rhymed in couplets: AABBCC in every stanza (fight/light, plain/rain, king/thing).
Central ideaStruggle makes strength. A tree (and a person) that grows without hardship stays small and ordinary; one that faces storms becomes strong, tall and wise.
Stanza 1A tree that never had to fight for light and air, standing safe in an open plain with enough rain, never becomes a "forest king" — it stays a stunted "scrubby thing".
Stanza 2The same is true of a man: one who never had to work hard for his land or share never becomes a "manly man"; he ends life exactly as he began.
Stanza 3The key stanza: good timber grows through hardship — "the stronger wind, the stronger trees"; "the more the storm, the more the strength". Both trees and men grow strong through sun, cold, rain and snow.
Stanza 4The finest, oldest trees (and men) are found where the forest is thickest, i.e. where competition is greatest. Their scars show many winds and struggles. "This is the common law of life."
Tone & moodEncouraging, instructive, optimistic and reflective; the mood is inspiring and hopeful.
Symbolstree = a person • open plain = comfort/easy life • thick forest = competition and challenge • wind, storm = difficulties in life • scars = experience and lessons from hardship • patriarchs = great, wise elders • stars = wisdom/high aspirations.
DevicesExtended metaphor (tree = man); parallelism / anaphora ("The stronger… the stronger…; the more… the more…"); personification ("hold counsel with the stars"); imagery (broken branches, scars); alliteration ("sun and sky", "stronger… storm… strength"); repetition ("sun and sky and light and air"); contrast between easy and hard growth; aphorism ("This is the common law of life").
MessageDo not fear challenges; they build character, strength and wisdom.

2024 C-Q1(b)(i)According to the poem, how can a person achieve his/her true potential in life? Explain with examples from the text. [5]

According to the poem, a person achieves his true potential by facing hardship and working hard. The poet says that "good timber does not grow with ease" and "the more the storm, the more the strength". The tree that grows in the thick forest and fights for light becomes a forest king, while the man who never toils "lived and died as he began". Therefore, challenges build strength and greatness.

2024 C-Q1(b)(ii)If you were a tree, how would you project yourself to be a strong and good one? Mention any TWO ways linked to the characteristics of a tree. [5]

If I were a tree, I would first grow deep roots. Like roots, strong values, discipline and education would keep me steady when storms of failure or bad influence come. Secondly, I would keep growing towards the light and give shade and fruit to others. In the same way, I would keep learning and use my talents to help my community.

2024 C-Q2(a)What do you think is the poet's purpose of writing this poem? Justify with TWO evidences from the poem. [10]

In my opinion, the poet's purpose is to motivate people to accept hardship as the path to strength and greatness. He wants readers to see difficulties not as punishment but as training for life.

The first evidence is in stanza 3: "The stronger wind, the stronger trees… The more the storm, the more the strength." These parallel lines show that the greater the challenge, the stronger a tree, and in the same way a human, becomes.

The second evidence is in stanzas 1 and 2. The tree that "never had to fight" and the man who "never had to toil" end up as "a scrubby thing" and a person who "lived and died as he began". The poet uses these negative examples to warn that an easy life produces no growth.

Finally, stanza 4 says that scars show "many winds and much of strife" and calls this "the common law of life". Thus, the poem urges us to face struggles with courage.

2024 C-Q2(b)How is a person's life similar to or different from the tree mentioned in the first stanza? Justify your stand with reference to the poem. [10]

A person's life is similar to the tree in the first stanza in one way but different in another.

It is similar because both need struggle to grow. The tree on the open plain "never had to fight" for sun, sky, air and light, and it remained "a scrubby thing". In stanza 2 the poet says the man who never had to toil "lived and died as he began". So a person who avoids effort and lives a comfortable life also stays small and achieves nothing.

It is different because a human being can choose and learn, but a tree cannot. A tree has to grow where its seed falls, but a person can leave comfort, take up challenges and change his own destiny. A tree's growth is also only physical, whereas a person grows in character, wisdom and courage, as the "scars" in stanza 4 suggest.

Therefore, a person's life resembles the tree only if he chooses an easy path.

Chapter 8: Poem — To My Mother (George Barker)Section C · asked in 2025 · a sonnet of praise and love

8.1 Must-know summary

AspectDetails
Poet & formGeorge Barker (English poet, 1913–1991). A sonnet of 14 lines, printed as 8 lines + 6 lines. The mother in the poem has an "Irish hand" (Irish roots) and the "bomber" shows it is set in wartime Britain (WWII).
Speaker & subjectA loving son speaks about his mother, who is both "most near" (in love) and "most far" (distant in time/space or perhaps dead).
Lines 1–8 (octave)The son's picture of his mother: sitting by the window, huge as Asia, shaking with laughter, enjoying gin and chicken, as funny as the writer Rabelais yet most tender towards weak creatures (lame dogs, hurt birds). She is such a magnificent "procession" that others can only follow behind her like a small dog behind a brass band.
Lines 9–14 (sestet)Her courage and calm in war: she will not look up at the bomber or run to a cellar, but stays like a mountain "whom only faith can move". The poet sends all his faith and love to tell her she will move from "mourning" into "morning" — from grief to hope.
Mood & toneAffectionate, admiring, tender, warm and humorous; the mood is nostalgic and loving, ending in hope.
Themeslove and gratitude for a mother • strength, humour and warmth of a mother • compassion for the weak • courage and faith in difficult times • hope after grief.
Rhyme (slant)Octave: far/for, found her/surround her, laughter/after, hand/band = ABCDABCD. Sestet: condescend/send, cellar/her, mountain/morning = EFGEFG.

2025 C-Q1(b)(i)Explain the first stanza of the poem in your own words. [5]

The speaker addresses his mother as the person nearest to his heart, yet far from him now. He remembers finding her by the window, huge and shaking with laughter, enjoying gin and chicken. She was as witty as Rabelais but very gentle with injured dogs and birds. She was such a grand personality that everyone else could only follow her humbly, like a small dog behind a brass band.

2025 C-Q1(b)(ii)List any TWO possible themes from the poem and explain how you can apply them to make your relationship better with people around you. [5]

Two themes are love and gratitude towards parents, and compassion for the weak. To apply the first, I would spend time with my parents, help at home and say thank you often. To apply the second, I would treat classmates and neighbours kindly, especially those who are weak or in trouble. These habits would build trust and warm relationships.

2025 C-Q2(a)The poem "To My Mother" is a sonnet. List any FOUR features of sonnet used in the poem and explain. [10]

A sonnet is a fourteen-line poem about a single idea. "To My Mother" shows four features of the form.

First, it has fourteen lines, printed as eight lines followed by six. Second, it is divided into an octave and a sestet. The octave (lines 1–8) paints the portrait of the mother, while the sestet (lines 9–14) deals with her courage and the son's faith. Third, there is a turn (volta) at line 9, "She will not glance up at the bomber", where the poem moves from a happy picture to her courage during war. Fourth, it follows a fixed rhyme pattern, ABCDABCD EFGEFG, using slant rhymes such as far/for, laughter/after and mountain/morning.

Finally, like a traditional sonnet, the poem develops one idea, love and admiration for the mother, and closes with a resolution: she will "move from mourning into morning".

2025 C-Q2(b)List any FOUR figures of speech used in the poem and explain how each contributes in conveying the messages effectively. [10]

The poet uses several figures of speech to show his love and admiration.

First, the oxymoron "most near… and most far" shows that the mother is close to his heart but distant, which creates a feeling of longing. Second, similes such as "as huge as Asia" and "like a mountain" make her appear large-hearted, powerful and unshakeable. Third, the metaphor "seismic with laughter" compares her laughter to an earthquake, showing her lively, joyful nature. Fourth, the allusion to Rabelais and to faith moving mountains tells us that she is witty and strong in faith. The apostrophe "O all my faith, and all my love" shows his deep emotion, and it also shows that she faces life with humour and courage.

The wordplay on "mourning" and "morning" in the last line carries the central message of hope. Together, these devices make the portrait vivid and the emotions deeply felt.
