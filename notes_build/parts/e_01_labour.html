Labour and its types

  • Labour = effort of any kind (manual or mental, skilled or unskilled) put in by human beings in the production of goods and services to earn income [[P25 Q1b(i): labour]]. The service is labour; the person is the labourer (a doctor is a labourer; treating a patient is labour).
  • Wage = payment by an employer to a worker for the services in production; an expense for the employer and an income for the worker; in cash or kind.

  • Dignity of labour: all jobs, manual or intellectual, are respectable and equally important; none is superior or inferior. Treating manual workers as inferior violates it [[P25 Q1a(xvii): A]].

Demand for labour

Demand for labour = number of workers an employer is willing and able to hire at a given wage rate in a given period.

  • Wage rate: inverse relation — wage falls, demand rises; wage rises, demand falls (downward-sloping curve). Table: Nu 600 → 1 worker, 500 → 2, 400 → 3, 300 → 4, 200 → 5.
  • Demand for goods and services: labour is wanted for the goods it makes, so it is a derived demand (demand that arises from the demand for other products) [[P24 Q1c(ii): e]]. A boom (more demand for goods) → firms expand → hire more labour [[P24 Q1a(viii)]] [[P24 Q5a]].
  • Productivity of labour: more productive (trained, skilled) workers contribute more, so demand rises; training, seminars and workshops raise productivity [[P25 Q5a]].
  • MRP: Marginal Product (MP) = extra output from one more worker = change in total production. MRP = MP × MR (price of one unit). A profit-maximising firm hires up to the point where MRP = wage (hire while MRP ≥ wage) [[P24 Q1a(xvii): D]].

Supply of labour

Supply of labour = number of people willing and able to work at the prevailing wage in a given period.

  • Wage rate: positive relation — higher wage, more workers supplied (Nu 500 → 1, 600 → 2, 700 → 3, 800 → 4, 900 → 5).
  • Facilities and working environment: good conditions, friendly colleagues, good management attract workers (supply rises); poor conditions make workers leave [[P24 Q1a(i): C]] [[P25 Q1a(viii): C]].
  • Other (for answers): qualifications and training needed, working hours, job security, benefits, population size, migration.

Equilibrium wage rate

  • Equilibrium is where the D and S curves meet: the equilibrium wage rate (OW) and equilibrium quantity (ON); quantity demanded = quantity supplied [[P25 Q1a(i): A]].
  • Above E (wage W1): excess supply of labour (unemployment) → wage falls. Below E (wage W2): excess demand (shortage) → wage rises.

Solved questions — Section B and short answers

Does a higher wage always raise the supply of labour? No, not always. (1) Workers also look at facilities and the working environment: a high wage with bad conditions or hostile colleagues may not attract workers. (2) Supply is limited by skills and qualifications required and by the number of people available, so more workers cannot appear at once (some prefer leisure at high wages). Professional labour is labour that needs advanced study and special training [1]. Example: a doctor treating a patient / an architect / a pilot [1]. (i) At Nu 1500 demand (10) > supply (6): A = Increase (excess demand pushes the wage up). At Nu 2500 supply (10) > demand (4): B = Excess supply. There is an inverse relationship: when the wage rate rises, firms hire fewer workers (demand falls); when the wage falls they hire more [1]. Yes, partly a skills mismatch: (1) many graduates have qualifications that do not match the skills employers need, so vacancies stay unfilled while youth stay jobless; (2) youth prefer white-collar/government jobs and avoid manual or technical work, while the available jobs are in these areas. (Other causes: too few jobs, COVID-19.) Investment in health gives healthy workers who are absent less, have more energy and live longer; investment in education and training gives knowledge and skills. Both make each worker produce more per hour (labour productivity), raise the MRP, wages and national income [1 + 1]. Labour in economics is effort put in to produce goods and services for payment or income [1]. A mother caring for her baby at home is unpaid, not sold in the market and not counted in national income, so she is not treated as labour — although the work is valuable to the family and society [1]. Two factors affecting the supply of labour: (1) the wage rate — a higher wage attracts more workers (positive relation); (2) facilities and the working environment — good conditions, friendly colleagues and management increase supply.
