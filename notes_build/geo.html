1 · The Universe & the Solar System
P25 Q1a(iii),b(iii),c(i),Q5b · P23 Q1a(i),d(iii),Q2d,Q4e

Key ideas

  • Universe = all space, matter and energy. Galaxy = billions of stars held by gravity (our Milky Way). Light-year = distance light travels in one year (≈ 9.46 trillion km).
  • Big Bang Theory: about 13.8 billion years ago the universe began as a single, extremely hot, dense point, then expanded and cooled; matter formed stars and galaxies. Put forward by the Belgian priest Georges Lemaître (1927) P25 Q1a(iii): A; Edwin Hubble (1929) showed galaxies are moving apart (not the theorist of the Big Bang).
  • Expansion of the universe means a wider gap between galaxies (space itself stretches; galaxies are not “new”) P23 Q1a(i): B.
  • Evidence: (1) red shift; (2) cosmic microwave background radiation (afterglow); (3) proportion of hydrogen (~75%) and helium (~25%).
  • Red shift = light from distant galaxies is stretched to longer (redder) wavelengths because they are moving away; the farther the galaxy, the bigger the shift → the universe is expanding → it must have been smaller and denser in the past. P23 Q2d
  • Solar Nebula Theory (Kant, Laplace): the solar system formed ~4.6 billion years ago from a rotating cloud of dust and gas (the nebula) P23 Q1d(iii): nebula. Gravity pulled the cloud in; it spun faster, flattened into a disc; the centre became the Sun.
  • Accretion: dust grains stuck together → planetesimals → they collided and merged into larger protoplanets → planets. The process is called accretion P25 Q1c(i). The Big Bang does not explain planets forming from dust and gas → 2025 b(iii) FALSE.
  • Planets: inner terrestrial (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) – small, rocky, because heat drove light gases away; outer giants (Jupiter, Saturn gas giants; Uranus, Neptune ice giants).

Solved questions

P25 Q5(b) [3] Why is the Solar Nebula Theory widely accepted? (1) It explains why all planets orbit the Sun in the same direction and almost the same plane (they formed from one spinning disc). (2) It explains the two kinds of planets — small rocky ones near the hot Sun, large gas/ice giants in the cold outer region. (3) Telescopes (and space probes) observe young stars surrounded by discs of dust and gas, and the theory fits the age of meteorites and Moon rocks (~4.6 billion years).
P23 Q2(d) [2] Red shift: meaning — the shift of light from a receding galaxy to longer (red) wavelengths, like the drop in pitch of a siren moving away. It explains that galaxies are moving away from each other, so the universe is expanding — evidence that it began from a single point (Big Bang).
P23 Q4(e) [2] Should Bhutan launch missions to planets like Mars? No, not at present. (1) Space missions are extremely expensive and need advanced technology and scientists that a small country does not have; (2) Bhutan’s money is better used for urgent needs — health, education, climate and disaster safety. (Bhutan can instead join international space cooperation, such as small satellites with partners. “Yes” is accepted if justified, e.g. inspiring youth and developing technology.)
Quick answers: P23 Q1a(i) B · d(iii) nebula. P25 Q1a(iii) A (Lemaître) · b(iii) FALSE · c(i) accretion.
2 · Earth, Plate Tectonics & Geological Time
P25 Q1a(vi),c(iv),Q2c,Q5a · P23 Q1a(iv,v),d(iv),Q2e,Q5a,e

Structure of the Earth

  • Crust (thin, solid; continental thicker, oceanic thinner) → mantle (~2,900 km, hot flowing rock) → outer core (liquid iron-nickel) → inner core (solid).
  • Lithosphere = crust + rigid top of mantle, broken into plates. These plates “look like wooden blocks floating over a weak, ductile region”, the asthenosphere P23 Q1a(v): D. Tuzo Wilson (1960s) gave the idea of plates of continental and oceanic crust.

Continental drift & plate tectonics

  • Alfred Wegener (1912): all continents were once joined as the supercontinent Pangaea (“all land”) ~200–300 million years ago, which split and drifted into present positions P25 Q1c(iv): Pangaea. Pangaea first split into Laurasia (north) and Gondwana (south).
  • Evidence: jigsaw fit of coasts; same fossils on separate continents; matching rock layers/mountains; glacial deposits in warm lands.
  • Plate boundaries: convergent (collide: fold mountains, trenches, volcanoes, earthquakes), divergent (move apart: rift valleys, mid-ocean ridges), transform (slide past: earthquakes).
  • Himalayas: the Indian plate moved north and converged with the Eurasian plate 50–70 million years ago; the sediments of the Tethys Sea between them were crumpled and folded upward, forming young fold mountains P23 Q1a(iv): C P25 Q1a(vi): B P23 Q1d(iv): fold mountains. Convergent movement presses both sides of the boundary and folds the rock layers. The plates are still pushing, so the Himalayas keep rising and Bhutan has earthquakes.
  • Proof the Himalayas were under the sea: (1) marine fossils (ammonites, shells) found in Himalayan rocks; (2) sedimentary rocks such as limestone and shale formed on the sea bed lie at high altitude; (3) salt deposits and the sea-floor rocks (ophiolites) found there. P23 Q2e

Geological Time Scale (GTS)

  • A chronological system dating Earth’s rock strata and history. Order from oldest: Precambrian → Palaeozoic → Mesozoic → Cenozoic P25 Q5a.

Solved questions

P25 Q5(a) [2] Chronological order: i. Precambrian → iii. Palaeozoic → ii. Mesozoic → iv. Cenozoic (i, iii, ii, iv).
P23 Q5(a) [2] FOUR events of the Cenozoic era (66 mya to today): (1) the Himalayas and Alps rose as the Indian plate collided with Eurasia; (2) mammals spread and became the dominant animals (Age of Mammals); (3) Ice Ages (Pleistocene) covered large areas and sea level rose and fell; (4) modern humans appeared; also the continents reached their present positions and grasslands spread.
P25 Q2(c) / P23 Q5(e) [2] If the continents stayed as one land mass (Pangaea) — TWO consequences: (1) very dry continental interior far from the sea: vast deserts and extreme hot summers/cold winters; (2) one huge ocean, shorter coastline → less coastal land, fewer marine habitats and less rainfall; also species would mix and some become extinct, and there would be no Himalayas or Indian Ocean as now. (Any two.)
P23 Q2(e) [2] The Himalayas were once under the sea — TWO evidences: (1) marine fossils such as ammonites and shells are found in the high rocks; (2) the rocks are marine sedimentary rocks (limestone, sandstone) deposited on the Tethys sea bed and later lifted when India collided with Eurasia.
Quick: P23 iv C, v D; d(iv) fold mountains. P25 vi B; c(iv) Pangaea.
3 · Landforms: Wind, Rivers & Underground Water (Karst)
P25 Q1d,Q7a,b · P23 Q1a(vi),Q6a,b,Q7e

Agents of gradation

  • Erosion (wearing away) → transportation (carrying) → deposition (dropping when energy is lost). Agents: running water, wind, glaciers, underground water, waves.
  • Deposition happens when the river loses energy and drops the material it was carrying P25 Q1d(ii): a — e.g. delta, floodplain, alluvial fan.

Work of wind (arid areas)

  • Erosion: deflation = wind lifts and blows away loose dust and sand P25 Q1d: f; abrasion = wind-carried particles strike and wear the rock surface (sand-blasting) P25 Q1d(iv): e.
  • Transportation P25 Q1d: suspension — fine soil particles lifted and held in the air (v: b); saltation — grains bounce along the ground up to about 1.5 m (iii: c); surface creep — larger, heavy particles pushed and rolled along the ground (i: d).
  • Erosional features: mushroom (pedestal) rocks, yardangs and zeugen (ridges), deflation hollows/ oases, desert pavement.
  • Depositional features: sand dunes (barchan = crescent-shaped; seif = long), loess (fine wind-blown silt, fertile).

Work of underground water — Karst

  • Karst topography = landscape of limestone (soluble rock) shaped by the dissolving action of underground water, with sinkholes, caves and underground drainage. P23 Q7e
  • Process: rainwater absorbs CO₂ → weak carbonic acid → dissolves limestone (calcium carbonate) along joints (solution).
  • Erosional features: lapies (grooved ridges), sinkholes/dolines (funnel-shaped hollows), swallow holes (where streams vanish underground), caves/caverns P23 Q1a(vi): B stalactite is NOT erosional.
  • Depositional features (dripping water leaves calcite): stalactites (hang from roof), stalagmites (grow up from floor), pillars/columns (when they join). P23 Q6a

Solved questions

P25 Q7(a) [2] How a stalagmite forms: water containing dissolved calcium bicarbonate drips from the cave roof; when a drop hits the floor it loses CO₂ and evaporates, leaving a small deposit of calcite. Drop after drop, the deposit builds upward into a cone. (If it meets a stalactite growing down, they form a pillar.)
P25 Q7(b) [2] TWO negative impacts of tourism on karst landscapes: (1) tourists touch, break or write on stalactites and stalagmites (they grow only ~1 cm in 100 years), and trample the surface; (2) waste, litter, oil and sewage pollute the underground water and cave, and lights/crowds disturb bats and cave life.
P23 Q6(a) [1+1] (i) Figure 1 shows a pillar (column) in a limestone cave. (ii) It forms when a stalactite growing down from the roof and a stalagmite growing up from the floor join, by repeated deposition of calcite from dripping water.
P23 Q6(b) [2] TWO features formed by wind in arid areas: (1) Mushroom rock – wind-blown sand abrades the base of a rock more than the top, giving a narrow stem and wide cap (erosion); (2) Sand dunes – wind drops sand where it loses speed, forming crescent-shaped barchans or long seif dunes (deposition). (Others: yardangs, loess, deflation hollows.)
P23 Q7(e) [1] Karst topography: the unusual landscape that develops on limestone when slightly acidic rainwater and underground water dissolve the rock, creating sinkholes, caves, underground streams and, where water drips, stalactites and stalagmites.
Quick: P25 d: i d · ii a · iii c · iv e · v b (f deflation and g erosion unused). P23 vi B.
4 · Climate, Vegetation & Houses of Bhutan
P25 Q1a(ii,v),c(ii,iii),Q2e,Q7d · P23 Q1a(iii),Q3b,Q5c,d,Q7d

Factors controlling Bhutan’s climate

  • Altitude (the main factor) — Bhutan rises from ~100 m to 7,500 m; temperature falls ~6.5 °C per 1000 m. High regions are cooler P25 Q1c(ii): lower. Gasa has cool summers and cold dry winters mainly because of its altitude P25 Q1a(v): C.
  • Mountains (direction/orientation of ranges): they block the monsoon winds — windward southern slopes get heavy rain (5000+ mm), leeward inner valleys and the north are dry (rain shadow); mountains force air to rise and cool, forming precipitation (orographic). Mountain ranges affect precipitation → 2025 b(iv) TRUE.
  • Monsoon: moist south-west monsoon winds from the Bay of Bengal bring most rain in June–September. The graph’s rainfall rise from January to May shows the approach of the summer monsoon P25 Q1a(ii): B.
  • Latitude (26.7°–28.3° N, sub-tropical) gives high sun angle, but altitude overrides it.
  • Seasons: winter (Dec–Feb), spring (Mar–May), summer monsoon (Jun–Sep), autumn (Oct–Nov).
climate graph

Climatic zones & vegetation

  • Alpine zone is hardly inhabited because the climate is cold throughout the year, the ground stays frozen most of the time and farming is not possible P23 Q1a(iii): D. Collecting valuable cordyceps (Balu-Sulu / yartsa gunbu) gives highlanders income, so it is not a reason for the zone to be uninhabited.
  • Altitude 200–2000 m = sub-tropical zone P25 Q1c(iii) (check zone names in your textbook).
  • Climate affects plants and animals: temperature and rainfall decide the vegetation type; animals adapt (thick fur, hibernation, migration) P23 Q3b.

5 · Latitude, Longitude & Time
P25 Q1a(iv),Q3e,Q4a,c · P23 Q1a(ii),c(i),Q3a,Q5b,Q7c

Key ideas

  • Latitude = angular distance north/south of the Equator (0°–90°); parallels. Longitude = angular distance east/west of the Prime Meridian (0°, Greenwich); meridians (0°–180°).
  • Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours → 15° = 1 hour → 1° = 4 minutes. East is ahead; west is behind.
  • Standard time: Bhutan 90° E → GMT + 6 h; India 82½° E → GMT + 5½ h; London (GMT) 0°; Singapore 104° E; Sydney 151° E.
  • Uses of latitude P25 Q4a: (1) locates a place and its climate zone (tropical, temperate, polar); (2) gives day length and seasons; (3) used with longitude for navigation; (4) measures north–south distance (1° ≈ 111 km).
  • GMT = mean solar time at Greenwich. Limitations P25 Q4c: based on one meridian only and the Sun, so it varies by place and season; Earth’s rotation is not perfectly steady, so it cannot meet the needs of atomic-precision timekeeping (GPS, internet, aviation), which now use UTC; it ignores daylight-saving changes and large countries using several zones.
  • GPS (Global Positioning System) is an advanced method of finding latitude and longitude using satellites → 2023 c(i) FALSE.

Solving time problems

  1. Find the longitude difference (subtract if both are E or both W; add if one is E and one W).
  2. Multiply by 4 minutes (or divide by 15 for hours).
  3. Add the time difference for a place to the east; subtract for a place to the west.

Solved questions

P25 Q1a(iv) [1] 9 AM in Bhutan → London: difference 6 h (Bhutan is ahead), so 9 − 6 = 3 AM (C).
P23 Q1a(ii) [1] Sydney 151° E, Bhutan 90° E: difference = 61° × 4 = 244 min = 4 h 04 min; Sydney is east (ahead): 5:00 a.m. + 4 h 04 = 9:04 a.m. (B).
P25 Q3(e) [2] Singapore time 5:48 PM, Delhi (77° E) 4:00 PM → Singapore is ahead by 1 h 48 min = 108 min. Longitude difference = 108 ÷ 4 = 27°. Singapore is east: 77° + 27° = 104° E.
P23 Q3(a) [2] Delhi (70° E) 8 p.m.; Place A 5 a.m. (next day) = 9 hours ahead → Place A is east. Difference = 9 × 15 = 135°. Longitude of A = 70° + 135° = 205° E = 155° W.

P25 Q4(a) [2] TWO uses of latitude: (1) it tells which climatic zone a place lies in (hot tropics, temperate, cold polar) and the length of day and seasons; (2) it is used to locate places and navigate (ships, aircraft) and to measure north–south distance.

6 · Map Reading & Survey Tools
P25 Q1a(i),b(i),e(i–ix),Q2a,Q5c · P23 Q1c(i),e(i–viii)

Topographic maps

  • A topographic map shows the natural and man-made features of an area in detail: relief (contours, spot heights), drainage, vegetation, settlements, roads, trails, boundaries. Survey of Bhutan maps are at 1 : 50,000.
  • Map numbering (India & adjacent countries series, IAC): degree sheet (e.g. 78) → divided into 16 sheets lettered A–P (1° × 1°) → each lettered sheet divided into 16 numbered 1–16 (15′ × 15′, the 1:50,000 map). A map is written e.g. 78M/3 (Mongar) or 78M/11 (Trashigang). The numbering of topographic maps is not the same in every country → 2025 b(i) FALSE.
  • Conventional signs and symbols (colours and small pictures) P23 Q1e(vii): blue = water, green = vegetation, brown = contours, black = man-made features, red = roads/settlements. They are important because: (1) they give a universal language so any user can read any map; (2) they save space and avoid clutter of words, so more information is shown clearly; (3) they let us identify features and routes quickly.

Scale

Grid references & contours

  • Four-figure reference = a grid square (e.g. 2030: easting 20, northing 30). Six-figure = an exact point: easting (2 digits + tenth) then northing (2 digits + tenth), e.g. 405502. Rule: “along the corridor, then up the stairs” — read the eastings (left→right) first, then the northings (bottom→top).
  • Contours join points of equal height. Contour interval = vertical gap between two contours (read from the map’s margin; every fifth is a thicker index contour). Close = steep; wide = gentle; V pointing uphill = valley; V pointing downhill = spur; spot height = a point with its height printed.
  • Drainage patterns: dendritic (tree-like), trellis (parallel main rivers with right-angled tributaries), radial (outward from a peak), parallel (steep uniform slope).

Measuring the length of a river

  1. Lay a thread carefully along every bend of the river on the map.
  2. Straighten it and measure with a ruler → map length (cm).
  3. Multiply by the scale: at 1:50,000, length (km) = cm × 0.5. Example: 8.6 cm × 0.5 = 4.3 km. P23 Q1e(iv) (map)

Survey and geospatial tools

  • Plane table survey: equipment = plane table (drawing board), tripod stand, alidade, spirit level, plumbing fork and plumb-bob, compass, drawing sheet, pins.
  • GPS (satellite positioning), GIS and Google Earth (satellite images, 3-D terrain, measuring tools).

8 · Population & Settlements
P25 Q1a(x),Q2b,Q3a,c,Q4b,e,Q6c · P23 Q1a(viii,ix),c(iii,v),Q3d,Q4a,b,Q7a

Key ideas

  • Population change = births − deaths + (immigrants − emigrants). Growth rate = % change per year. Bhutan’s rate fell from 1.09% (2012) to 0.64% (2022); the world’s rate from 1.19% (2015) to 0.91% (2024). Projected Bhutan population: 727,100 (2017) → 797,300 (2027) → 883,900 (2047).
  • Population projection lets planners estimate how demographics will develop (schools, jobs, housing) → 2023 c(iii) TRUE.
  • Life expectancy (70.2 years in 2017) rose through better healthcare, education and living standards/clean water P25 Q1a(x): D = i, ii, iv. As it rises, the mortality (death) rate decreases P23 Q1a(ix): B.
  • Distribution: uneven — dense in the west (Thimphu, Paro, Chukha) and southern foothills; sparse in the high north and east. Causes: relief, climate, roads, jobs and services.
  • Migration: internal (within the country, e.g. village to village in the same dzongkhag, rural → urban) P23 Q1c(v): TRUE; international (to other countries). Push factors (poverty, no jobs, wildlife damage) and pull factors (jobs, services). Gungtong = households left empty when families move out of villages.
  • Central Place Theory (Walter Christaller, 1933): a town is a central place serving its surrounding area; low-order goods (salt, rice) are bought at the nearest small place so people travel short distances for them, high-order goods (hospital, university, car) are bought at larger towns, so people travel farther. Hexagonal market areas. Threshold (minimum demand) and range (maximum distance people travel).
  • Incorrect statement about CPT: “the number of larger towns and smaller towns is roughly equal” — in fact smaller towns are many, larger towns few P23 Q1a(viii): D. Correct: same-size towns evenly spaced; removing a town readjusts the system; larger towns are spaced farther apart.

9 · Agriculture, Land & Food Security
P25 Q2e,Q3b,Q4d,Q5e · P23 Q1a(vii),d(v),Q3c,Q6d

Key ideas

  • Subsistence farming = small-scale farming for the family’s own food using primitive tools and human/animal labour (the past type in Bhutan) P23 Q1d(v): subsistence. Commercial farming = large-scale, machinery, hybrid seeds, for sale.
  • Land fragmentation = farm land divided into small scattered plots as it is shared among children. Best method: farm cooperatives (pool land and work together) P23 Q1a(vii): A; also land consolidation.
  • Food self-sufficiency: Bhutan still imports rice, edible oil and vegetables. Challenges: shortage of farm labour (rural–urban migration), human–wildlife conflict, small fragmented steep fields, limited irrigation, climate change, pests. Ways: farm mechanisation, electric fencing, irrigation, cooperatives, markets, climate-smart methods, youth in agriculture.
  • Pests: fall armyworm (maize, since 2019) and Giant African Land Snail (vegetables, since 2010). Strategies: Integrated Pest Management: early monitoring and reporting, pheromone/light traps, hand-picking and destroying, biological control (natural enemies, neem), crop rotation/early planting, quarantine and farmer training.
  • Climate-smart agriculture (CSA): seed saving, crop rotation, terracing, water harvesting, agroforestry. It raises productivity by keeping soil fertile, saving water and soil and reducing risk from weather.

12 · Natural Disasters & Safety
P25 Q1a(ix),c(v),Q3d,Q6b,e · P23 Q1a(x),d(ii),Q2c,Q4d,Q7b

Key ideas

  • Hazard = a threat; disaster = a hazard causing great loss of life and property. Natural: earthquake, flood, landslide, GLOF, storm, forest fire. Disaster management cycle: preparedness, response, recovery, mitigation.
  • Earthquakes: Bhutan lies in an active seismic zone because the Indian plate presses against Eurasia (e.g. 2009 Mongar earthquake). Before: secure heavy furniture, prepare a kit, practise Drop–Cover–Hold On; during: drop, take cover under a strong table and hold on, stay away from glass and walls, in the open go to open ground; after: expect aftershocks.
  • Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF): a lake dammed by moraine breaks suddenly. The Lugge Tsho GLOF of 1994 caused heavy damage in Punakha P25 Q1a(ix): D. The disaster caused by retreating glaciers due to global warming is a GLOF P25 Q1c(v). Seismic activity is a natural trigger, not a human-made cause (human-made: global warming, deforestation, industries) P23 Q1a(x): B.
  • Flash floods and landslides in the southern foothills: very heavy monsoon rain (5000+ mm), steep slopes with young, fragile Siwalik rocks and loose soil, and deforestation / road construction; short steep rivers rise very quickly.
  • Forest fires: >1,400 incidents in ten years; mostly human-caused in dry winter/spring.
  • Electric fires: unplugging appliances when not in use and safe wiring reduce fire disasters P23 Q1d(ii): fire.
  • Education in Emergency (EiE): restarting teaching, play and counselling soon after a disaster.

Solved questions

P25 Q3(d) [2] TWO methods of fighting forest fires: (1) prevention and early detection — fire lines/breaks, watch towers, patrols, drones/satellite alerts, awareness and bans on open burning; (2) quick suppression — trained community fire-fighting teams with beaters and water, backed by the forest department and the use of controlled burning of fuel before dry season.
P25 Q6(b) [2] Earthquake-safe buildings: (1) follow the building code: strong foundation, reinforced concrete bands/ring beams at floor and roof level, and simple symmetrical shapes; (2) use light, flexible materials (timber frames, light roofs), avoid heavy roofs and soft storeys, and retrofit old buildings; traditional dhajji / ekra walls are flexible.
P25 Q6(e) [2] Technology and life-saving skills in disasters: (1) early-warning systems, phone alerts, social media and apps warn and guide people quickly; (2) GPS, drones, thermal cameras and satellite images locate victims and assess damage for rescue; also first-aid videos and online training.
P23 Q2(c) [2] ONE strategy for earthquakes: Drop, Cover and Hold On during the shaking — drop to the floor, cover your head and neck under a sturdy table and hold on until it stops; before: keep an emergency kit and know safe spots and exits and fix heavy furniture to walls.
P23 Q4(d) [2] Flash floods and landslides in the southern foothills: (1) very heavy monsoon rainfall quickly fills the short, steep rivers; (2) steep slopes with loose, weak Siwalik rock and soil, made worse by deforestation and road cutting, slide down easily.
P23 Q7(b) [3] “Education in emergency should be carried out by every school.” Agree. (1) It keeps learning going, so children do not lose a year of education; (2) schooling gives safety, routine and psychosocial support, helping children cope with fear and loss; (3) children are protected from abuse, child labour and early marriage, and schools can teach disaster awareness and act as shelters/centres for the community.
